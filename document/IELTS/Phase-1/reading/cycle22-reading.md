## Bài 1 — The Observer's Notebook (110 từ)

May, a psychology student, keeps a small notebook in her bag. In the notebook, she writes observations from daily life.

"Monday, on the bus: a man in a black jacket kept clenching his jaw. Traffic tension, maybe."

"Wednesday, at the university: my professor walked with his hands behind his back — confidence in his territory."

"Friday, in a café on Hang Bac street: two friends leaned in at a distance of 40 centimeters. Close friendship, or a secret."

Her teacher says: "Don't interpret immediately. Describe first, interpret later. Write what you see in the moment, at the scene. The conclusion can wait a day."

In one month, May filled a whole notebook. Her conclusion at the end: "People are less mysterious than I thought. They announce themselves everywhere — in their walk, in their hands, at every bus stop and café table. We just never look."

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What does May write in her notebook?
2. What is her teacher's advice?
3. What is May's conclusion after a month?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Observations of body language from daily life (with day + place).
2. Describe first, interpret later — conclusions can wait.
3. People announce themselves everywhere (in walk, hands, at every stop/table) — we just never look.

</details>

## Bài 2 — Different Countries, Different Bodies (120 từ)

Body language has no international dictionary.

The thumbs-up means "good" in America — but it is an insult in parts of the Middle East. The "OK" sign means money in Japan, zero in France, and something rude in Brazil. In Bulgaria, nodding your head means NO — imagine the confusion at a business meeting in Sofia.

Personal space is cultural too. In Latin countries, people stand close — 50 centimeters feels friendly. In Northern Europe, the same distance feels like an invasion; they back away to a full meter.

Eye contact differs as well. In the US, a firm gaze shows honesty. In Japan and Vietnam, too much eye contact with an elder feels disrespectful; eyes go down as a sign of respect.

The lesson: before you read someone's body, read their culture. The same gesture, in a different country, tells a different story.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What does nodding mean in Bulgaria?
2. How does personal space differ between Latin and Northern European countries?
3. What is the lesson of the text?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. NO (the opposite of most countries!).
2. Latin: close (50cm) feels friendly; Northern Europe: back away to a meter (too close feels invasive).
3. Before you read someone's body, read their culture — the same gesture tells a different story.

</details>

## Bài 3 — The Baseline Rule (110 từ)

Here is the most important rule of reading people: find the baseline first.

The baseline is a person's normal — how they behave when they are relaxed, honest, and comfortable. Some people never make eye contact; that's just who they are. Some people always fidget; that's their engine. Some people naturally speak with flat tone; that's their music.

Only after you know the baseline can you notice changes. A quiet person going quiet — nothing new. A loud person going quiet — something is happening.

It's like knowing the weather in a city. In Hanoi, rain is normal in June. In the desert, one cloud is news.

So don't judge people against a universal standard. Judge them against themselves. That's not just psychology — that's fairness.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is a baseline?
2. Why is 'a loud person going quiet' more meaningful than 'a quiet person going quiet'?
3. What is the city weather comparison about?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. A person's normal behavior when relaxed/honest/comfortable.
2. Because it's a CHANGE from their baseline — quietness is new for them.
3. Rain is normal in Hanoi in June but news in the desert — you need context (the local baseline) to judge what's significant.

</details>
