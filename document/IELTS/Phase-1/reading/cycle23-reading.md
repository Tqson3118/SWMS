## Bài 1 — The Poker Player's Secret (110 từ)

My uncle played poker for twenty years, and he taught me one lesson that works everywhere.

"Amateurs look for tells," he said, "but professionals look for changes."

A tell is a famous sign — touching the nose, looking up. But smart players know these famous signs, so they fake them or hide them. What they cannot fake is change. If a player who talks all night suddenly goes silent, something happened. If a player who sits still starts moving, something happened.

"People can control their faces," Uncle said, "because they practice their expressions every day in the mirror. But few people pay attention to their feet. When someone feels uncomfortable or is bluffing, their feet often turn toward the door — non-verbal studies suggest this reflects an unconscious tendency to disengage from tension."

I don't play poker, but I use his rule in every meeting: watch the feet, and watch the changes.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is the difference between amateurs and professionals?
2. Why can't players easily fake 'changes'?
3. What body part do people rarely practice — and what can it reveal?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Amateurs look for famous isolated tells; professionals look for shifts and changes in baseline behavior.
2. A change is often involuntary — it happens when something real shifts in their emotional state.
3. The feet — they often angle toward an exit when someone feels tense or detached (unconscious disengagement cue).

</details>

## Bài 2 — The Doctor's Handshake (110 từ)

A young doctor noticed something strange: patients lied to him more than to the older doctors.

Why? Because his body language invited lies. He looked at his computer while asking questions, so patients felt unheard. He stood by the door, so patients felt he was leaving. He asked "You're taking the medicine, right?" in a suspicious tone — so patients said yes, although they weren't.

He changed three things. He sat down, so conversations became unhurried. He asked open questions like "How are the pills treating you?", because open questions invite honest stories. And he stayed silent two seconds longer, because silence gives truth time to arrive.

Honesty isn't only about character. It's also about the environment you create. If you want truth, your body must first say: "I can handle it."

**Câu hỏi:**
1. Why did patients lie more to the young doctor?
2. What three things did he change?
3. What is the big lesson?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. His body language invited lies — looked at computer, stood by door, suspicious tone (patients felt unheard/judged).
2. He sat down; asked open questions; stayed silent longer.
3. Honesty is also about the environment you create — your body must first say "I can handle it."

</details>

## Bài 3 — Mirroring (110 từ)

Watch two old friends at a café. One leans forward, and a minute later, the other leans forward too. One touches her hair, and the other touches her hair. They are mirroring — copying each other's bodies without knowing it.

Psychologists found that we mirror people we like or want to connect with. It's automatic, and it's ancient: babies mirror their mothers' faces at three weeks old. Mirroring says, silently: "I am with you. We are the same."

Salespeople and politicians sometimes use mirroring deliberately — copying a client's posture to build trust. Does it work? Only when it's subtle. Obvious copying feels like mockery, and mockery destroys the very trust it wanted to build.

The natural version, though, is beautiful: friendship written in body language, at every café table in the world.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is mirroring?
2. Why do we mirror people (and since when)?
3. When does deliberate mirroring fail?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Copying each other's body language without knowing it.
2. To connect with people we like — automatic and ancient (babies do it at 3 weeks).
3. When it's obvious — it feels like mockery and destroys trust.

</details>
