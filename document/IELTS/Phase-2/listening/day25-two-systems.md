### Questions (source-dependent)

1. How does the talk distinguish the experiencing self from the remembering self?
2. What does the remembering self do after an experience ends?
3. What does the focusing illusion distort?
4. Why can a story's ending affect the remembered evaluation of the whole experience?

<details>
<summary>Hidden answer key (open after attempting)</summary>

1. The experiencing self lives through events in the present; the remembering self evaluates the story afterward.
2. It stores and judges the experience as a remembered story rather than adding every moment equally.
3. It distorts the importance we assign to circumstances affecting well-being.
4. The remembering self gives disproportionate weight to how the experience ends.

- **Official transcript:** https://ed.ted.com/lessons/the-psychology-behind-irrational-decisions-sara-garofalo (official lesson page and transcript notes).
- The lesson explains cognitive processes in judgment. This task uses transcript evidence only; no audio playback, audibility, duration, pronunciation, intonation, CEFR, or learner-performance claim is made. Listen to the talk and attempt the questions before opening the key. If playback is unavailable, use the text for reading and record listening as blocked.

### Questions (source-dependent)

1. How does the lesson distinguish automatic thinking from deliberate reasoning in decision-making?
2. What cognitive bias leads people to mistake conjunctions of events as more profitable than single events?
3. In what evolutionary context were quick heuristic judgments advantageous?
4. How can awareness of cognitive biases assist individuals in making better choices?

<details>
<summary>Hidden answer key (open after attempting)</summary>

1. Automatic thinking relies on fast pattern recognition, whereas deliberate reasoning requires focused mental effort.
2. The conjunction fallacy, where vivid descriptions override basic probability rules.
3. In ancestral environments where immediate threat detection prioritized speed over deep logical deduction.
4. By prompting people to pause, reflect, and evaluate objective data rather than trusting initial impulses.

</details>
