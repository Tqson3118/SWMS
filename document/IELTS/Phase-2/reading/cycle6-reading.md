## Bài 1 — What the Past Is For (bài cuối, ~450 từ)

Near the end of his life, a journalist asked the writer Gabriel García Márquez what he had learned about memory after a lifetime of writing about it.

He said: "Memory is not for keeping things. Memory is for making things."

I have thought about that sentence for years, and this month — this month of looking backward, of past tenses and childhood rooms and grandmothers' hands — seems the right time to pass it on.

Look at what we have been carrying. The defense mechanisms: walls we built when we were too small to build anything else. The attachment styles: maps drawn by infants, still guiding adults through cities the infants never saw. The inner child: waiting downstairs, patient as furniture. The stories we tell: not lies about the past, but architecture — the load-bearing walls of the person we currently are.

Here is the error, and we all make it. We think the work of the past is archaeology: dig carefully, catalogue every pain, and the truth will set you free. Sometimes it does. But sometimes archaeology just becomes inventory — a museum of grievances where we walk the same halls daily, dusting the same exhibits: look, there is the father's silence; there, the summer that changed; there, the message sent at 1 a.m.

Memory is not for keeping things. Memory is for making things.

The past you can use has three jobs. First, evidence: you survived things, and the proof is you, reading this page. Second, material: every scar is a story, and stories, well told, become bridges to other people — the entire reason humans bother telling each other anything. Third, direction: the wound shows where the work is. The fear of abandonment points straight at the practice of trust. The inner child points at whatever you needed at seven — go give it to someone. That's the whole map.

And when the past refuses to be useful — some of it won't, some of it is just weather that happened — you are allowed to put it down. Not forgive, not forget, not resolve. Put down. There is a shelf for finished things.

You have spent this month learning to speak about what was. That was never the goal. The goal was to free the verbs — was, were, used to, would — so they stop ruling you and start serving you. A person fluent in their past is not a person trapped in it. They are a person with a very deep toolbox.

The child downstairs is still there. So is the author with the pen. Introduce them. That conversation is the rest of your life.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did García Márquez say memory is for?
2. What are the three jobs of the useful past?
3. When the past refuses to be useful, what are we allowed to do?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Not for keeping things — for MAKING things.
2. Evidence (you survived), material (scars become stories become bridges), direction (the wound shows where the work is).
3. Put it down — not forgive, not forget, not resolve; there is a shelf for finished things.
