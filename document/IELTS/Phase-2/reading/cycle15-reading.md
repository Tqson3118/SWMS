## Bài 1 — The First Small Yes (~390 từ)

In 1966, two researchers **knocked** on doors in California asking residents to put a large, ugly "Drive Carefully" sign on their lawn. Almost everyone said no — 17% said yes. A different street was approached differently: residents were first asked to sign a small petition about safe driving. Two weeks later, the same people were asked for the big ugly sign. Forty-seven percent said yes — almost three times as many.

The trick has a name: the **foot-in-the-door technique**. If you can get one small "yes", a later, much bigger request becomes easier. Salespeople sell small first; cults recruit one evening at a time; fundraisers ask for the newsletter signup before the donation. The mechanism is the one Cialdini **had** named *commitment and consistency*: once you have agreed to be the kind of person who cares about safe driving, refusing the lawn sign would make you a hypocrite in your own eyes.

There is an older, meaner cousin. Ask for something so large that the target clearly refuses — then retreat to the small thing you actually wanted. "Will you volunteer ten hours a week? No? What about a single two-hour shift at the event?" This is the **door-in-the-face technique**, and it works because the retreat feels like a concession, and the recipient feels a debt for it: a gift of compromise demands a gift in return.

A third cousin is **low-balling**: agree to a price, then — just before signing — add a small fee. Because the buyer has already mentally committed, most absorb the change rather than restart the negotiation. Car dealerships **have** run this play for half a century.

What unites all three? They weaponize the gap between *who you think you are* and *what you are about to do*. The foot gets in because the person inside has agreed, in some small way, that they are already on your side. The face does not slam the door because the concession feels like a kindness.

Your defense is not to refuse all small requests — that would make you a hermit. The defense is to notice the pattern: small-then-large, retreat-to-small, agree-then-add. When a request grows — or shrinks — at exactly the moment of your resistance, you are inside a technique. The way out is to ask, out loud, of yourself: *would I have said yes to this final request, if I had never been asked the first one?*

That single second question — rewind to the clean version — is the entire alarm system.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the 1966 California study find, and what is the technique called?
2. Describe door-in-the-face and low-balling in one sentence each.
3. What is the single "alarm system" question that defeats all three?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Residents who first signed a small petition later agreed to the big ugly sign 47% of the time vs 17% without; foot-in-the-door technique.
2. Door-in-the-face: ask for something too big, then retreat to what you actually wanted. Low-balling: agree on a price, then add a small fee just before signing.
3. "Would I have said yes to this final request if I had never been asked the first one?" — rewinding to the clean version.

</details>

## Bài 2 — The Story Two Facts Tell (~360 từ)

In 1957, a researcher **paid** subjects one dollar or twenty dollars to lie to the next subject that a boring task was actually fun. The twenty-dollar liars felt fine — they had a good reason. The one-dollar liars felt terrible, and something strange happened: their actual opinion of the task **moved**. They started to genuinely believe the task had been interesting. They had talked themselves into the truth of their own lie.

This is **cognitive dissonance** — the discomfort of holding two contradictory beliefs, and the mind's loyal work to remove the discomfort by editing one of them. If I lied, and I am not the kind of person who lies for nothing, then the lie must be true. If I chose this job over a better offer, the job must be the better offer. If I paid a fortune for this course, the course must be worth a fortune. The brain, a quiet accountant, balances the books by rewriting the inventory.

The finding travels far. Cults that predict the end of the world on Tuesday — and are wrong — do not, on Wednesday, abandon the cult. The disconfirmed prophecy makes them believe harder; the greater the sacrifice, the louder the rewrite. Smokers who know the science know it less clearly the more they smoke; the cost of the contradiction is too high, so the belief bends instead.

If you are honest with yourself, you have caught this accountant at work in your own life — the relationship you stayed in past the evidence because leaving meant admitting you had been wrong for years; the belief you cannot quite release because it cost you too much to hold.

There is no vaccine against cognitive dissonance — it is the sound of a mind trying to be consistent with itself, which is mostly a virtue. What you can do is notice the rewrite at the moment it happens. When a choice feels too costly to reconsider, ask the question out loud: *if I had known then what I know now, would I have chosen this?* If the honest answer is no — the discomfort you feel is not evidence that you are right. It is the sound of the accountant, panicking.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What happened to the one-dollar liars that did NOT happen to the twenty-dollar liars?
2. What is cognitive dissonance, and what does the mind do to remove the discomfort?
3. Give the two real-world examples of dissonance-driven belief-bending from the article.

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Their actual opinion of the boring task moved — they started to genuinely believe the task had been interesting.
2. Discomfort from holding contradictory beliefs; the mind removes it by editing one belief to be consistent (the brain balances the books by rewriting the inventory).
3. Cults that predict the end of the world and are wrong believe harder; smokers who know the science know it less clearly the more they smoke.

</details>

## Bài 3 — What Would You Have Done? (~370 từ)

Every famous social psychology experiment ends with the same unspoken question: would you have done better? The honest answer — uncomfortable but useful — is probably not.

If you **had been** a Milgram subject, the odds say you would have pressed the buttons. If you **had been** in Asch's room, you would have, at least once, denied your eyes. If you **had watched** Kitty Genovese fall, you might have watched, too, in your window, assuming someone else would call. The literature is one long, polite mirror: we are not the people we predict we will be inside the experiment.

Here is why that matters, and it is not to depress you. The literature has also, quietly, mapped every off-ramp. Milgram's obedience falls when a peer refuses. Asch's conformity collapses when one actor answers honestly first. The bystander effect shatters the moment one person moves. Zimbardo's prison ended the day an outsider asked a simple question. *The spell is real and the spell is breakable — but only by someone who knows the spell exists.*

That is the entire gift of this field, in one sentence: knowing the bias does not erase it, but it gives you the pause in which it can be refused. And the most important thing to refuse is not the conformity or the obedience in others — it is the certainty in yourself. The person who reads Milgram and says "I would never have obeyed" is exactly the person who, statistically, would have. The person who reads it and says "I might have. Let me see how the breaks are built" is the person who, sometimes, refuses.

If you **were** in the next experiment — and you are, every day, in a smaller one — your best move is to walk in already knowing that you can be bent. That is not weakness; it is the precondition of the only kind of strength that survives a real room. The naive strength that says "I would never" breaks against the first polite authority. The trained strength that says "I might, and here is my exit" sometimes walks out.

Every famous experiment ends the same way: with you. The only question that ever mattered is the one in the title.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What does the literature predict you would have done if you had been a Milgram or Asch subject?
2. What is the difference between the person who says "I would never have obeyed" and the person who says "I might have"?
3. According to the article, what is the "entire gift" of social psychology?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. You would probably have obeyed (Milgram) and, at least once, denied your eyes (Asch); we are not the people we predict we will be.
2. The "I would never" person is statistically the one who would obey; the "I might have" person is the one who sometimes refuses.
3. Knowing the bias does not erase it, but gives you the pause in which it can be refused.

</details>
