## Bài 1 — Thirty-Eight Witnesses (~400 từ)

In 1964, a young woman named Kitty Genovese **was** attacked outside her apartment building in New York. The attack lasted half an hour. Newspapers later reported that thirty-eight witnesses **had** watched from their windows — and nobody called the police.

The number was wrong, the story was half legend, but the question underneath it was real, and it launched a whole field: why do good people do nothing?

Two psychologists, Bibb Latané and John Darley, **had** started testing. They staged emergencies: smoke filling a room, a person falling, a woman crying for help. The pattern repeated everywhere. If a subject sits alone, he helps — quickly, almost every time, around 85%. But if he sits in a group of strangers, help collapses; if the group is large, it can fall below a third. They **called** it the **bystander effect**, and the engine inside it **diffusion of responsibility**: if ten people can hear the same cry, each one's share of duty becomes one tenth — and one tenth of an obligation feels like zero.

There was a second engine, stranger than the first. In the smoke experiment, when subjects sat with passive actors, many **sat** in a fog that could have killed them, looking at each other. If nobody reacts, the situation must not be an emergency — the room full of calm faces becomes its own evidence. We read danger in other people's faces before we read it in the smoke.

Now the good news, buried in the same data. If one person moves, the spell shatters: one helper **creates** helpers; the second and third follow almost instantly. And there is a practical trick — the one piece of advice this whole field has earned: if you are ever the victim in a crowd, do not shout at the crowd. Point at one person: *you, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance.* A named person has one hundred percent of the responsibility, and one hundred percent is a weight humans will carry.

The thirty-eight windows, it turned out, **had** held fewer watchers than the headlines claimed — some called, some shouted, some simply did not understand. But the lesson survives its own legend: in a group, the line between doing nothing and doing something is thinner than we think — and one voice, one name, one pointing finger is all it takes to cross it.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What happens to helping rates when a person is alone vs. in a group?
2. What are the two engines of the bystander effect?
3. What is the practical advice for a victim in a crowd, and why does it work?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Alone: ~85% help quickly; in a group, help collapses (can fall below a third in large groups).
2. Diffusion of responsibility (each person's share feels like zero) and social reading (calm faces become evidence that nothing is wrong).
3. Point at one specific person and give them a task — a named person carries 100% of the responsibility, which humans will act on.

</details>

## Bài 2 — The Room Where No One Says No (~390 từ)

In January 1986, the space shuttle Challenger **lifted** off and broke apart seventy-three seconds later, live on television, with a schoolteacher aboard. The weather was freezing. Engineers **had** warned, in writing, the night before: the O-rings fail in cold. The warning went into a meeting, and the meeting produced a launch decision.

What happened in that room is the textbook case of **groupthink** — the term psychologist Irving Janis **had** coined for a very specific failure: when a group wants unity more than accuracy. The symptoms are so predictable you can print them as a checklist: illusions of invulnerability; self-appointed mindguards who filter bad news before it reaches the leader; pressure on doubters, politely applied ("let's not relitigate this"); and — the quiet killer — self-censorship, the doubter who **says** nothing because his doubt feels disloyal.

Notice what groupthink is NOT. It is not stupidity — NASA **had** the smartest engineers on Earth in that room. It is not evil — nobody wanted the shuttle to explode. It is a side effect of a thing we usually praise: cohesion. A tight team runs smoothly; a too-tight team runs smoothly off a cliff. If agreement feels warm and doubt feels like betrayal, the group is already sick.

The cures exist, and they are almost embarrassingly mechanical. Leaders **speak** last, not first — otherwise the meeting becomes a referendum on the boss's opinion. Every important decision gets a assigned devil's advocate, someone whose job is to argue the other side. Groups **split** into sub-groups to think separately, then reassemble. And outside reviewers — people who owe the group nothing — get the raw material before the decision, not the summary after it.

You have sat in that room. The family plan that everyone secretly doubts; the project meeting where the timeline is fantasy and the whole table nods; the group chat that turns into an echo with thumbs. In each of them, one honest sentence costs a moment of comfort and buys back reality.

Seven astronauts and a teacher **had** trusted a room full of smart, loyal, warm people who could not say one small word: *wait*.

That is the whole job description of a devil's advocate. One word. Your turn to volunteer.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is groupthink, and what did the Challenger case have in common with it?
2. What is groupthink NOT — and why is cohesion a double-edged sword?
3. Name three mechanical cures for groupthink mentioned in the article.

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Wanting unity more than accuracy; engineers' written cold-weather warning was neutralized in a cohesive meeting that produced the launch decision.
2. Not stupidity, not evil — a side effect of cohesion; smooth teams can run smoothly off a cliff when agreement feels warm and doubt feels like betrayal.
3. Leaders speak last; an assigned devil's advocate; splitting into sub-groups; outside reviewers get raw material before the decision (any three).

</details>

## Bài 3 — Alone in a Uniform (~370 từ)

In 1969, researchers **asked** children to shout as loud as they could — once alone, once in groups. In groups, each child shouted at about two-thirds volume. The finding replicated with adults, clapping, pulling ropes, typing, thinking: put people in a group, hide their individual output, and effort quietly drops. Psychologists **called** it **social loafing** — we pull harder when our pull is visible.

The mirror image is **deindividuation**. In Zimbardo's Stanford study, "guards" wore matching uniforms and mirrored sunglasses; their names were never used. In Ku Klux Klan robes, in halloween masks, in anonymous comment sections — the pattern holds: strip away the individual identity, and behavior drifts toward whatever the costume permits. The uniform **becomes** the moral license.

Read those two findings together and you have the operating system of almost every crowd in history. Social loafing: if my contribution is invisible, I contribute less. Deindividuation: if my identity is invisible, I do things "I" would never do. Anonymity, in both directions, is the solvent.

But notice the lever hiding inside both findings — visibility. Make the individual visible again, and the machine reverses. Swimmers **swim** faster in relays when their split times are posted. When workers **sign** their work — a real name on the weld, on the commit, on the report — quality rises. Online communities that require persistent identities **behave** better than anonymous ones, holding everything else constant. The trick is older than psychology: guilds **stamped** their makers' marks into swords for a reason.

So if you lead a team, the question is never "how do I make them work harder?" The question is: where is output currently anonymous? If three people "share" a responsibility, three people own a third of it; if each name sits next to each number, each person owns their number completely.

And if you are IN the crowd — uniformed, anonymous, carried — the question is sharper: what is my name, and am I doing what it would sign?

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the shouting experiment find, and what is it called?
2. What is deindividuation, and what does anonymity do to behavior in both findings?
3. What is the "lever" that reverses the machine, with examples?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Children shouted at ~2/3 volume in groups; social loafing — effort drops when individual output is hidden.
2. Losing individual identity in uniforms/masks makes people do what the costume permits; anonymity dissolves both effort and responsibility.
3. Visibility — posting split times, signing work with real names, persistent online identities, makers' marks on swords.

</details>
