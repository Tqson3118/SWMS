# 📖 Đọc 3 — Attachment Theory (~420 từ)

> Bowlby & Ainsworth đơn giản hóa — vì sao cách ta yêu bắt đầu từ tuổi 0.

---

Why do some people feel safe in love, while others constantly fear abandonment? In the 1950s, psychologist John Bowlby proposed an answer that changed everything: our adult relationships are built on our first relationship — the one we had before we could speak.

A baby cannot survive alone. So evolution gave babies one job: keep the caregiver close. When the caregiver responds warmly and reliably, the baby learns: "When I need help, help comes." This is called a **secure attachment** — a calm foundation under the personality.

But what if the caregiver is inconsistent — sometimes warm, sometimes absent? The baby learns: "Help sometimes comes." The result is an **anxious attachment**: an adult who loves deeply but worries constantly. Is she angry? Did that text mean something? The anxious lover checks, asks, holds tighter — not from weakness, but from an old alarm system that never fully turned off.

And if the caregiver is cold or rejecting? The baby learns: "Help does not come." The result is an **avoidant attachment**: an adult who feels safest at a distance. "I don't need anyone" — but underneath, the need is there, buried in a locked room.

In the 1970s, Mary Ainsworth tested this with the famous "Strange Situation": babies played in a room while their mothers briefly left and returned. Secure babies cried, then calmed and returned to playing. Anxious babies cried and could not calm down. Avoidant babies didn't cry — but their little hearts were racing on the monitors.

Here is the hopeful part: attachment style is not a life sentence. Psychologists call earned security the slow process of re-learning safety — through a patient partner, a good friend, a therapist, or even through honest self-reflection. The brain that learned fear can learn trust.
