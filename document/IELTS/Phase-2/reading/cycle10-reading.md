## Bài 1 — The 3 a.m. Cart (~380 từ)

At 3 a.m. last March, I **bought** an air fryer I did not need, in a color I did not like, for a kitchen I did not yet have. The box **has been sitting** unopened in my hallway since.

Nothing was wrong with the air fryer. The problem was the hour. Studies of online shopping **have shown** for years that decisions made between midnight and 4 a.m. follow a simple rule: tired brains want to feel better right now. The cart is not a shopping list at that hour — it is a small emergency room. Something in the day **has gone** unprocessed, and the brain reaches for the cheapest available medicine: a button that says *Buy now*.

My grandmother understood this without any research. She **had** a rule her whole life: never make a big decision after the sun went down. "The morning mind is a different judge," she said. Science **has been confirming** her for fifty years. Sleepy judges have been handing out harsher sentences, sleepy buyers have been clicking "upgrade", sleepy lovers have been sending the email that they cannot unsay.

This is not weakness. It is biology. The prefrontal cortex — the brain's brakes — **has been losing steam** since about 9 p.m., while the emotional brain keeps its full power until morning. By 3 a.m., you are driving a car with no brakes on a road with curves. The car still goes. You just cannot stop it where you want.

The fix is mechanical and almost stupid. Take any 3 a.m. cart and close the laptop. Say: "I **will look** at this again at 9 a.m." The morning you — the one with brakes — **has been winning** that negotiation, in my experience, about eighty percent of the time. The twenty percent? Those are the things the daytime-me actually wanted, and forgot to admit.

I opened the box last week, finally. The air fryer works. But the lesson the hallway box **has been teaching** me for a year — that the buyer at 3 a.m. is not the buyer at 9 a.m., and only one of them should be trusted with my card — is worth more than the appliance ever was.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. Why do decisions made between midnight and 4 a.m. tend to be bad?
2. What was the grandmother's rule, and what has science said about it?
3. What is the practical fix, and how often does it work for the author?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Tired brains want to feel better right now; the prefrontal cortex (the brakes) is weak late at night while the emotional brain keeps full power.
2. Never make a big decision after sundown — science confirms sleepy judges/buyers/lovers make worse calls.
3. Close the laptop and revisit at 9 a.m.; it works about 80% of the time (the daytime self rejects the purchase).

</details>

## Bài 2 — Everyone's Highlight Reel (~390 từ)

In 2019 I **went** to a wedding, and a man I had not seen in ten years asked me, over cold cuts: "So, what have you been up to?" I told him — work, a book, the dog. He told me — a promotion, a new house, two marathons. I drove home and felt, for the first time in a calm life, a strange green weather I could not name.

I know the word now. Envy. And the engine that runs it is a quiet comparison the mind **has been making** since childhood, every time two people meet.

The trouble is that we compare against *highlight reels*. People do not bring their worst footage to weddings — they bring the marathons, the promotions, the clever lines they rehearsed on the drive over. My neighbor's disaster of a marriage I **have heard** about only from his wife, never from him. The friend who "looks so happy on Instagram" **has been writing** me sad emails at 2 a.m. for a year. The reels are real — they are just a cut of the truth.

Psychologists **have been warning** us for decades that the comparison game has no winning square, because the field is always full of people one step ahead of you on something. There is always a faster runner, a bigger house, a cleverer child. The brain, however, does not compare us to "people generally". It compares us to whoever stood in front of us last, in their Sunday clothes.

There is a way out, and it is not "stop comparing" — that is like telling a fish to stop noticing water. The way out is to choose what you compare. I **have been running** my life against two baselines for three years now: the me of five years ago, and the me I hope to be in five years. The first one I always beat; the second one I almost never reach, and that gap — measured by me, against me — is the only one that **has ever told** me anything true.

At the next wedding, someone will still ask the question. Let them. Just remember, as the cold cuts are passed: this is the reel, not the film.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the author feel after the wedding, and what is its engine?
2. Why is comparing ourselves to others a game with "no winning square"?
3. What two baselines has the author chosen to compare against instead?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Envy; its engine is the quiet comparison the mind makes whenever two people meet.
2. Because there is always someone ahead on something — faster runner, bigger house, cleverer child — and the brain compares to whoever stood in front of us last.
3. The "me of five years ago" (always beaten) and the "me I hope to be in five years" (almost never reached) — measuring self against self.

</details>

## Bài 3 — The Paralysis of Ninety Drinks (~370 từ)

There is a coffee shop near my office with ninety-one drinks on the menu. I **have ordered** the same one every morning for two years. The owner once asked me, kindly, why I never try anything else. I had no answer that day. I have one now: choice overload.

A famous experiment **has been sitting** in my head since I read it. A grocery store set up a tasting table — one day with twenty-four jams, one day with six. More people stopped at the twenty-four. But ten times more people actually *bought* jam on the six-jam day. The bigger display froze the buyers; the smaller one moved them.

This paradox — more options, fewer decisions, less satisfaction — **has been appearing** everywhere researchers look. Retirement plans: employees offered many funds enroll *less* than those offered few. Dating apps: people with hundreds of "matches" go on fewer dates. After twenty options or so, the brain stops choosing and starts panicking — and then it chooses the first familiar thing, because the familiar thing is the exit.

Why? Because every choice costs energy. Decision scientists **have called** the cost *decision fatigue*, and it is why your phone is full of unused apps you meant to delete, why judges get harsher before lunch, why you cannot pick a movie at 10 p.m. on a screen of two thousand titles. The tank is empty. The default wins.

The defensive move is almost embarrassing in its simplicity: cut the menu yourself. Pick three restaurants for the week, three outfits for work, one coffee order. The freedom is not in having ninety-one drinks; it is in having decided, once, that ninety-one is too many.

My coffee shop owner **has been** quietly teaching this for two years. I just thought she was loyal. She was — to her own sanity. I have started copying her. So far, nothing **has been lost** except a tired kind of freedom I never actually wanted.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the jam experiment show?
2. What is "decision fatigue", and what are two examples of it?
3. What is the practical defense against choice overload?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. A bigger display (24 jams) attracted more browsers, but the smaller display (6 jams) produced ten times more actual purchases.
2. The energy cost of choosing — it's why we don't delete apps, why judges get harsher before lunch, why we can't pick a movie among 2,000 titles.
3. Cut the menu yourself — pick three restaurants, three outfits, one coffee order; decide once that 91 is too many.

</details>
