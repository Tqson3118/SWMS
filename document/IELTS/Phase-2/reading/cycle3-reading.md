# 📖 Đọc — Chu Kỳ 3 (Day 9-11): quá khứ vs hiện tại

> 3 bài dùng used to / would dày đặc — nhắm mắt nghe nhịp "used to".

## Bài 1 — The Man I Used to Be (~370 từ)

My father used to be a strict man. Dinner was at six exactly. Grades below eight received silence — his silence was louder than any punishment. We used to eat in near quiet, four people chewing carefully around a table, hoping the rice would last forever because after dinner came homework and after homework came bed, and tomorrow was the same day again.

He used to check my math homework with a red pen. Every error got a circle, and the circles had a sound — a small scrape of disappointment. At twelve, I hated that pen.

When I was sixteen, he changed. It happened fast — my grandmother, his mother, died in March. He came back from the funeral a different size. Not smaller exactly, but rearranged.

The red pen disappeared. In its place, questions appeared: "What do you think?" — a sentence he had never used before, awkward in his mouth like a foreign phrase. He would sit in the kitchen while I studied, not checking anything, just being there, reading his newspaper upside down sometimes because he was watching me instead of the words.

Last month, I was cleaning his desk (he is eighty now, and I clean things he can pretend he still cleans) when I found a box of my old homework. Every page — twelve years of math, every circle of the red pen — kept. Not thrown away. Filed.

And between the pages, folded small, a note in his handwriting from 1998, the year of the circles: "Must be more patient. He is trying. The pen scares him. Tomorrow — no pen."

There was no tomorrow, that year. The pen came back for four more years. But there was the note — proof that a strict man was fighting a war inside himself, in a language of circles and silence, and losing, and knowing he was losing.

My father used to be a strict man. Now he is an old man who files his son's homework for thirty years and writes secret notes to himself about patience. Both men are my father. The circles, the silence, the box, the upside-down newspaper — when people ask me what my childhood was like, that is my answer: it was all of that, at the same time.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What used to happen at dinner, and after dinner?
2. What changed the father at sixteen — and how?
3. What did the writer find in the box, and what did it prove?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Dinner at six exactly, near silence; after: homework then bed, same every day.
2. His mother (grandmother) died in March; he came back rearranged — red pen replaced by questions ("What do you think?").
3. All 12 years of homework kept/filed + a 1998 note: "Must be more patient... Tomorrow — no pen." Proof the strict man was secretly fighting himself.

</details>

## Bài 2 — Grandmother's Hands (~360 từ)

My grandmother's hands used to be the fastest things in our village. That is not poetry; it is testimony. She could clean a fish in nine seconds. She would make rice while talking, listening to two radio stations, and watching the gate for my grandfather's bicycle.

When I was small, I would sit beside her and try to copy her hands. "Slower," she would say, laughing. "The rice doesn't run away." But her hands never learned slowness — they had six children to feed through three floods and one war. Speed was not a talent; it was survival that stayed after the emergency left.

She is ninety-four now. Last week, I was feeding her soup when she looked at her own hands lying in her lap — thin, spotted, still — and said something I will keep forever.

"These hands used to be quick."

"I know, Grandma."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You don't know. You think quick means busy. Quick meant I loved you faster. When you cried, I was already there. When you were hungry, the rice was already cooking. Slow hands love too — like yours now, feeding me. But my love was fast. It arrived before you asked."

**Câu hỏi:**
1. Why were the grandmother's hands fast — talent or something else?
2. What did she mean by "my love was fast"?
3. How does the writer feed her now — and what does that echo?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Survival — six children, three floods, one war; speed stayed after the emergency left.
2. Her love arrived before you asked — she was already there when you cried; rice already cooking when hungry.
3. The writer now feeds her slowly — slow hands love too (the care cycles back).

</details>

## Bài 3 — The Town That Used to Be Ours (~340 từ)

We used to own this town — every child believes that. The bakery on Hai Ba Trung was "ours" because the owner knew our names. The river bend was "ours" because we had buried a time capsule there in 1999, four ten-year-olds certain that the future would want to know us.

I went back last month. The bakery is a phone shop. The owner's daughter runs it now; she has her mother's eyes and none of her mother's bread. I bought a phone charger and asked, carefully, if she remembered the kids who used to buy the sesame cakes after school.

"My mother talked about you," she said. "The loud one, the quiet one, the one who cried when the cakes sold out, and the one who paid with buttons."

I was the one who paid with buttons.

The river bend was still there. Rivers are loyal that way. I was digging for the capsule for two hours before I remembered: we never buried it. We were ten; we spent the whole afternoon arguing about what to put in it — a photo? a coin? a letter to the future? — and then it got dark, and we went home for dinner, full of plans for next Saturday.

There was no next Saturday. One of us moved away that summer. Then another. The capsule stayed an idea, and ideas don't survive in the ground anyway.

But here is the thing I realized, sitting on the riverbank with dirty hands: we didn't need the capsule. The town remembered us without it. The bakery remembered the button-money. The river remembered nothing, and that was also a kind of kindness.

We used to own this town. It turns out the town owned us right back — it kept the parts of us that mattered, and let the river take the rest.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What four things did the bakery's daughter remember?
2. Why did the writer dig for two hours — and what was the truth about the capsule?
3. What did the writer realize on the riverbank?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. The loud one, the quiet one, the one who cried when cakes sold out, the one who paid with buttons.
2. He forgot they never actually buried it — they spent the afternoon arguing and went home; there was no next Saturday.
3. They didn't need the capsule — the town remembered them anyway (the town owned us right back).

</details>
