# 📖 Đọc — Chu Kỳ 2 (Day 5-7): khi ký ức quay lại

> 3 bài ~350-450 từ. Mỗi ngày 1 bài. Chú ý Past Continuous làm nền.

---

## Bài 1 — The Song on the Bus (~360 từ)

It was a normal Tuesday. I was sitting on the bus home, tired after work, watching the city slide past the window. Then, without warning, a song started playing from someone's phone two rows behind me.

It was a song from my university years — a silly pop song that my old roommate used to play every morning while she was getting dressed. For three years, that song was the soundtrack of my twenties.

The memories flooded back. I wasn't on the bus anymore; I was in that small rented room with the broken air conditioner. My roommate was singing badly into a hairbrush. We were eating instant noodles at 1 a.m., dreaming about futures that, in the end, arrived completely differently from our plans.

The song lasted three minutes. When it ended, I was back on the bus — older, with gray in my hair and a mortgage on my phone's calendar. I checked her contact. We hadn't spoken in four years. Not from a fight — just the slow, quiet distance that adult life builds between people who once shared everything.

That evening, I sent her a message: "A song on the bus made me think of you. Are you still singing badly?"

She replied in eleven seconds: "Worse than ever. When are you visiting?"

Psychologists say nostalgia is the mind's way of telling us what mattered. I don't know if that's science or poetry. I only know this: a stranger's phone gave me back a friendship on a Tuesday evening. Sometimes the past doesn't haunt us. Sometimes it just taps us on the shoulder and says: remember what matters.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What was the writer doing when the song started?
2. How did the friendship end — a fight or something else?
3. What did the writer do that evening?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Sitting on the bus home, tired, watching the city (Past Continuous background).
2. Not a fight — just slow, quiet distance built by adult life.
3. Sent a message to the old roommate — and got a warm reply in 11 seconds.

</details>

## Bài 2 — The House That Memory Built (~380 từ)

They say you can't visit the same house twice — because the second time, you are different.

Last month, I was walking through my old neighborhood when I decided to visit my childhood home. The gate was smaller than I remembered. The guava tree that my brother used to climb was gone — cut down, probably, by some family that came after us.

A woman was watering the plants in what used to be our yard. I didn't go in. I was standing at the gate when she noticed me.

"Can I help you?" she asked, kindly.

"I used to live here," I said. "Forty years ago."

She smiled and invited me in anyway. The rooms were the same and completely different. My small bedroom — where I used to hide comic books under the mattress — was now an office. The kitchen, where my mother was always cooking something, smelled of different spices now.

But here is the strange part: the floor in the hallway had one loose tile. Forty years, two families, and the tile was still loose — still making that little click sound that I used to think was the house talking to me at night.

I stepped on it. Click.

The woman laughed. "We never fixed it," she said. "It feels like the house's heartbeat."

On the walk home, I thought about memory. We think we remember places, but really we remember ourselves in places. The house was just a box. The home was my mother's cooking, my brother's laughter from the guava tree, and a loose tile that said goodnight.

The woman is still there now, watering plants, being somebody else's memory. One day, some child of hers will stand at that gate, forty years from now, looking for a heartbeat under a loose tile.

I hope they find it.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the writer used to do in the small bedroom?
2. What was unchanged after 40 years?
3. What did the writer realize about memory on the walk home?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Hide comic books under the mattress.
2. The loose tile in the hallway (still clicking).
3. We don't remember places — we remember ourselves in places (the house was just a box; the home was the people/sounds).

</details>

## Bài 3 — Two Letters to My Younger Self (~350 từ)

At twenty-five, I wrote a letter to my fifteen-year-old self. I found it again at forty.

"Dear me," the letter began. "Stop worrying about what they think of you. In ten years, you won't remember their names." That was true — I had to check old yearbooks to recall one single face.

"Learn to save money." Also true. The years after twenty-five taught me that lesson the hard way.

But here was the line that hurt: "Stop being so sensitive. Grow thicker skin."

At fifteen, I was ashamed of my sensitivity. I cried at films. I felt other people's bad moods like weather. I thought it was a defect — so did every adult around me, who kept saying the same thing: toughen up.

So the twenty-five-year-old me, still believing them, wrote that instruction to the child. Grow thicker skin.

Twenty-five years of living later, I know better. Sensitivity was never the defect. The people who told me to toughen up were not describing a better way to live — they were describing a different way, one that worked for them.

My sensitivity is why I notice when a friend's voice changes on the phone. It's why I write. It's why strangers tell me their stories on buses and trains — something in them recognizes something in me. Thicker skin would have protected me from some pain, yes. It would also have deafened me to all of this.

So now I am writing a second letter — forty to fifteen:

"Dear me. Keep the thin skin. The world will tell you to harden; the world is loud, but it is not always right. Your softness is not a crack in you. It is a window. People will climb through it to find you — and you will spend your whole life grateful that they did."

I keep both letters in the same drawer. One is from a young man trying to survive. One is from an older man, finally learning to live.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the 25-year-old tell his younger self about sensitivity?
2. What changed in the writer's view at 40?
3. What does the writer call sensitivity in the second letter?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Stop being so sensitive / grow thicker skin (toughen up).
2. He realized sensitivity was never a defect — it's how he connects with people (notices friends, writes, strangers share stories).
3. A window (not a crack) — people climb through it to find him.

</details>
