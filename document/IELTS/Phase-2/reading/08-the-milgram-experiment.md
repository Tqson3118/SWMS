# 📖 Đọc 2 — The Milgram Experiment (~370 từ)

> Chủ đề: **obedience** — con người thường vâng lời đến đâu khi quyền lực đứng sau. Đánh dấu các câu Past Perfect và Past Simple xen kẽ (2 tầng chuyện).

---

Yale University, 1961. A man in a gray lab coat **tells** a volunteer: the experiment continues; you must go on. The volunteer is pressing buttons labeled from "mild shock" to "XXX — danger: severe". Behind the wall, a "learner" — an actor, though the volunteer doesn't know it — screams, then begs, then falls silent. The volunteer turns to the man in gray. Sweat on his face, he asks the question that made this experiment famous: *who is responsible if something happens to him?*

The man in gray **had** prepared his answer in advance, like a script for four different endings: "the experiment requires that you continue." Nothing about right or wrong. Just a requirement.

Before the experiment, Milgram **had** asked psychiatrists to predict how many subjects would go all the way to the maximum shock. The experts said about one in a thousand — sadists, essentially, a rare species. The real number was twenty-six of forty. Sixty-five percent of ordinary people — teachers, workers, fathers — pushed the last button on a machine they believed was hurting a screaming human being, because a calm stranger in a lab coat said the experiment required it.

Notice what was NOT there. No threats. No weapons. No punishment for refusing. When people refused, nothing happened to them — Milgram **had** built no consequences at all. The only materials in that room were a quiet voice, a technical phrase, and a gray coat that looked like authority.

Since 1961, the study **has** been replicated many times, in many countries, with the same ugly stability: about two thirds follow orders past their own moral alarm. The percentage barely moves. What moves it — down, dramatically — is what Asch found: one fellow subject who refuses, an experimenter who is unsure, orders given by phone instead of in person. Authority, it turns out, is a spell that needs witnesses.

The learner, remember, was an actor; the shocks were fake. But Milgram's debriefing **had** left scars of a different kind: many subjects **learned** something about themselves in that basement that they never fully forgot. And so did the rest of us, reading the numbers from a safe distance — one in a thousand, we think, not me.

The gray coat is asking you something today, too. It just wears different clothes now.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the psychiatrists predict before the experiment, and what was the real number?
2. What was NOT in the room that makes the result frightening?
3. What three things "move the percentage down"?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. They predicted about 1 in 1,000 would go to the maximum; the real number was 26/40 (65%).
2. No threats, no weapons, no punishment for refusing — only a calm voice, a technical phrase, and a lab coat.
3. A fellow subject who refuses; an unsure experimenter; orders given by phone instead of in person.
