## Bài 1 — The Room You Grew Up In (~380 từ)

A patient once told me: "I don't understand why small things destroy me. My partner is ten minutes late, and something in me decides the relationship is over. Rationally, I know ten minutes means traffic. But the feeling arrives before the thinking, and the feeling is always louder."

She grew up with a father who traveled for work — sometimes two weeks, sometimes two months. There was no schedule, no warning. He would appear at breakfast with gifts and stories, and she would be happy for exactly one day, because she had already learned the pattern: the gifts meant the leaving had already been planned.

"By the time I was eight," she told me, "I had a system. When he came home, I started missing him in advance. It hurt less that way. I was pre-grieving, like a little accountant of loss."

This is what psychologists mean when they say the past lives in the present. Her adult self knows traffic exists. But her nervous system — the eight-year-old accountant inside her — runs on 1997 software: love arrives, therefore departure is coming, therefore start the pain early, in small manageable amounts.

The ten minutes aren't ten minutes. They are two months of childhood, compressed.

Here is what she said after a year of therapy, and I offer it to whoever needs it: "I can't uninstall the software. But I've learned to see it running. Now, when the feeling arrives, I say: ah, this is eight-year-old me, doing her math. And I hold her hand — the feeling, I mean — while my adult brain checks the actual facts. Traffic. Just traffic. The fear was real once. It's just not real now."

The room you grew up in is still inside you. Sometimes, on quiet evenings, you can hear its floorboards settle. That is not a malfunction. That is a person who survived a house, telling you about it — and asking, finally, to be heard rather than obeyed.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What was the patient's childhood system, and why?
2. What does "the ten minutes aren't ten minutes" mean?
3. What did she learn after a year of therapy?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Pre-grieving: when her father came home, she started missing him in advance — it hurt less (she expected the leaving).
2. Ten minutes of lateness triggers/compresses two months of childhood abandonment — the reaction is to old pain, not present facts.
3. She can't uninstall the software, but she can SEE it running — hold the feeling's hand while her adult brain checks actual facts.

</details>

## Bài 2 — Resilience Is Not a Rock (~360 từ)

People imagine resilience as a rock — something hard, unbreakable, that storms hit and storms lose. The image is wrong, and it harms the very people it pretends to praise.

Resilience is not a rock. Resilience is water.

Watch what water does in a flood. It doesn't stand and fight. It moves. It goes around the obstacle, rises when needed, goes quiet when wise, and — this is the part we forget — it always, always finds its way back to the sea. Water is never ashamed of detours. A river that bends is not a failed river.

I learned this from a friend who lost everything at forty-one: job, marriage, health — the full trilogy. For the first year, he tried to be a rock. He refused help, refused tears, refused to move back to his hometown, because rocks don't retreat and he thought resilience meant standing in the same place, getting hit, proudly.

The turning point came, of all places, in a hospital waiting room. An old man next to him was waiting for dialysis results, chatting calmly about his tomato garden. "You look like a man trying to hold a door shut," the old man said. "I've been where you are. The door leads to a room called 'the way things were.' There's nothing in there anymore, son. The living is out here."

My friend moved back to his hometown that spring. He teaches mathematics now — badly paid, he says, gloriously. He calls it the year he stopped being a rock and started being a river.

Here is the test I offer you: when hardship comes, ask not "How do I stand unmoved?" but "Where does the water go?" Resilience is not the refusal to bend. It is the refusal to stop flowing — around, over, quietly under, whatever the terrain requires. A rock survives by resisting. Water survives by continuing.

Be water. The sea is patient, and it remembers every river that ever found it.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is the wrong image of resilience, according to the text?
2. What was the turning point for the friend?
3. What question should we ask when hardship comes?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. A rock — hard, unbreakable, standing unmoved (it harms people by demanding proud resistance).
2. In a hospital waiting room — an old man told him the door leads to a room with nothing in it; "the living is out here." (He moved home, teaches, "stopped being a rock.")
3. Not "How do I stand unmoved?" but "Where does the water go?" (refuse to stop flowing).

</details>

## Bài 3 — The Inner Child (~350 từ)

There is a child in your house that you keep forgetting.

Not a real child — the one you used to be. Psychologists call this the inner child: the part of you that still holds your earliest feelings about being safe, loved, seen, or not. You grew up; the child didn't. The child just went downstairs and waited.

Sometimes you can hear the child without knowing. Why does one critical comment from a stranger ruin your whole afternoon, while other people shrug? Perhaps because, at seven, someone's voice taught you that being imperfect meant being unlovable. The adult reads an email; the child hears the old voice. You call it "over-sensitivity." It is actually archaeology.

The writer Alice Miller spent her career on a single idea: what we cannot remember, we repeat. The parent who was hit becomes, without noticing, a parent who hits — not from evil, but because the wound never found its words, and what has no words has no alternatives.

So how do you talk to a child who lives downstairs inside you?

Not with logic — children don't run on logic. Not with orders — you have tried "stop being ridiculous" on yourself; notice how well it worked. You talk to the inner child the way you would talk to any frightened child: gently. "I know. That was scary. I'm here now. You're not in trouble. You never were."

It feels ridiculous — until the evening it doesn't. Until the evening you say it, and something in your chest that has been holding its breath since 1998 finally exhales.

You are the adult in the house now. That comes with a duty nobody warns you about: the child downstairs is yours. Feed them. Learn their name. Ask what they were waiting to tell you.

They have been patient. God, they have been patient.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. Why does one critical comment ruin an afternoon — what's the real explanation?
2. What is Alice Miller's single idea?
3. How do you talk to the inner child?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Not over-sensitivity — archaeology: the adult reads an email, but the child hears the old voice from age seven.
2. "What we cannot remember, we repeat" — unworded wounds have no alternatives (patterns pass down).
3. Gently, like any frightened child: "I know. That was scary. I'm here now. You're not in trouble." — not logic, not orders.

</details>
