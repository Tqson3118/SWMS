## Bài 1 — The Boys of Robbers Cave (~410 từ)

In 1954, twenty-two eleven-year-old boys **arrived** at a summer camp in Oklahoma. They did not know they were inside an experiment, and for the first week, they did not even know the other group existed. Each group **had been** brought in separately; each named itself — the Rattlers and the Eagles.

Muzafer Sherif, the psychologist running it, **had** designed the camp as a machine for making prejudice. First phase: build cohesion. Second phase: friction — a tournament of baseball, treasure hunts, tug-of-war, with the scores announced and small provocations arranged. Within days, the Eagles and Rattlers **were** raiding each other's cabins, burning flags, carrying sticks. Boys who **had** never met called each other "dirty" and "cheaters". Asked to describe the other group, they produced a full catalogue of stereotypes about children identical to themselves.

Phase three was the famous part. Sherif tried the things adults always try first: contact, and speeches about getting along. They didn't work. Eating in the same hall, the boys threw food. Moral appeals bounced off eleven-year-olds the way they bounce off nations.

What worked was a series of **superordinate goals** — problems that (a) both groups needed solved, and (b) neither group could solve alone. The camp's water supply "broke" and the boys had to inspect a mile of pipe together. The truck "stalled" and both teams pulled on the same rope. On the bus home — the same bus, by the end — the Eagles and Rattlers **sat** together and sang.

Sherif's conclusion **has** aged into one of the steepest ideas in social science: intergroup hostility does not need history, race, religion, or reason. It needs only two groups and one scarce prize. Everything else — the hatred, the stereotypes, the flags — grows by itself, like mold in a damp room. And the cure is not speeches; it is a shared problem just big enough that neither side can pretend it doesn't need the other.

Look around the nearest conflict you know — office departments, sports fans, political tribes, online wars. The pattern rarely varies: two groups, one prize (budget, status, being right), a tournament someone arranged, and stereotypes that wrote themselves. Nobody needed to teach the boys of Robbers Cave to hate. Somebody only needed to keep score.

If the world's groups sat down to a water pipe that only they together could fix, we **would** call it naive. It has worked on every group it has been tried on, from boys to executives to post-war villages. The engineering is not mysterious. The scarcity of pipes is the only real shortage.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. How was the friction between Rattlers and Eagles created in phase two?
2. What failed before the superordinate goals worked?
3. What is Sherif's "steepest" conclusion about intergroup hostility?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. A tournament (baseball, treasure hunts, tug-of-war) with announced scores and small arranged provocations.
2. Contact and moral speeches — shared meals ended in food fights; appeals about getting along didn't work.
3. Hostility needs only two groups and one scarce prize — hatred and stereotypes grow by themselves; the cure is a shared problem neither side can solve alone.

</details>

## Bài 2 — The Label That Thinks (~360 từ)

A stereotype is a file the brain keeps about a group — fast, compressed, and written by someone who never checked it. The file saves time; that is its whole job. If you meet a hundred strangers, categories decide, in milliseconds, whom to approach and whom to watch. Without compression, a mind would drown.

The trouble begins where the convenience ends. First, the file writes itself from biased samples — news reports, jokes, one bad afternoon with one person **extended** to a whole category. Second, the file defends itself: the **confirmation bias** you met in month 2 collects each supporting case and quietly loses each disconfirming one, so the stereotype grows more "obvious" the longer it lives. Third — the cruel part — the file leaks into the labeled. Girls **told** that girls are bad at math do measurably worse on the next test; the label, spoken into the room, becomes a performance. Psychologists **call** it **stereotype threat**, and it works in milliseconds and in every direction.

Prejudice is the feeling that comes from the file; discrimination is the action the feeling buys. The three **are** separable, and the separation matters: a person can hold a biased file and still act fairly — if they know the file exists. That is the entire practical difference between a mind that is merely human and one that is dangerous: awareness, not purity. Nobody carries a clean file. Everybody carries a loaded one.

The only reliable maintenance is contact of a particular kind — not contact in general (Sherif's food-fights showed that), but contact as equals, on shared tasks, repeatedly, with individual names attached. When the label "they" acquires a face, a story, and a shared rope to pull, the file begins to be rewritten by data it cannot ignore.

You will not uninstall your stereotypes; the brain keeps its files. But you can do something the file cannot: when you feel the certainty arrive — "those people are all..." — you can pause and ask the file one question it has never once been asked: *who wrote you, and when did anyone last check?*

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What are the three troubles with stereotypes as described in the article?
2. What is stereotype threat?
3. What kind of contact rewrites the file, and what cannot?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Written from biased samples; defends itself via confirmation bias; leaks into the labeled (stereotype threat).
2. When a label is made salient, the labeled people perform worse in that exact domain.
3. Contact as equals on shared tasks, repeated, with individual names; mere contact in general does not (food-fights).

</details>

## Bài 3 — One Blue Eye (~370 từ)

The history of social psychology has a quiet punchline: change usually arrives by minority. Copernicus was one voice; the first abolitionists were a lunatic fringe; every right women now hold was once a minority position held by people society called crazy. Groups do not change from the center — they change from an edge that refuses to negotiate with the room.

Serge Moscovici **turned** this into an experiment in 1969. Subjects **saw** a series of blue slides and said, aloud, what color they saw. Among them sat two confederates who called the blue slides — every one, every time — *green*. That is all it took. In a striking share of trials, real subjects began answering "green" to blue slides; more of them drifted toward "maybe blue-green"; and afterwards, in private tests, their actual color perception **had** shifted. Two consistent voices, against a unanimous room, moved both the answers and the eyes.

The conditions Moscovii identified are precise, and they are the user manual for every changemaker: **consistency** — the minority must repeat the same position without wobbling; **confidence without rigidity** — firm tone, flexible when the majority concedes anything; and **not obviously self-interested** — the message must look like truth-seeking, not advantage-seeking.

Notice how different this is from the Asch story. In Asch's room, one honest voice broke conformity because the majority's answer was *wrong about the world*. In Moscovici's room, the minority voice moved the world itself — perception, in the private eye of the perceiver. The first is the power of accuracy; the second is the power of definition. Between them they cover most of history: someone insists the line is longer than the crowd says (Asch), and someone insists the slide is green until eyes begin to see it (Moscovici).

You do not need to start a movement. You need one room, one consistent sentence, and the stomach to repeat it while the majority smiles. "I see it differently." Said clearly, said kindly, said again next week. Most of the time, you will lose the room that day. And then, slowly, the room moves — because that is what rooms do when an edge keeps insisting.

Your difference from Moscovici's confederates: you have to believe it too.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What happened in Moscovici's 1969 experiment?
2. What three conditions make minority influence work?
3. What is the difference between Asch's honest dissenter and Moscovici's consistent minority?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Two confederates consistently called blue slides "green"; real subjects began answering "green" and their private color perception shifted.
2. Consistency; confidence without rigidity; not obviously self-interested.
3. Asch's voice was accurate against a wrong majority (power of accuracy); Moscovici's minority moved perception itself (power of definition).

</details>
