## Bài 1 — Sleep On It (~370 từ)

The best decision advice I **have received** in forty years was three words long, and it came from my first boss: *"Sleep on it."*

I was twenty-three, holding a resignation letter I **had drafted** at 9 p.m. after a terrible day. He read it, folded it, put it in his drawer, and said he would return it in the morning if I still wanted it. In the morning, I wanted it about forty percent less. By Friday, I wanted my job back completely. What changed overnight was not the office — it was me, twice over.

First, sleep **has been doing** something researchers call *memory consolidation* forever: the brain files the day, keeps what matters, and drops the heat from what merely burned. The humiliation that filled the whole sky at 9 p.m. is a small cloud by 7 a.m. — same event, different weather.

Second, and this is the part nobody tells you: the sleeping brain **keeps working**. Psychologists **have found** that people solve insight puzzles better after sleep, even when they do not remember dreaming about them. Some part of you spends the night testing arguments with nobody's ego in the room. You wake up with a conclusion and mistake it for a mood.

The tool has limits. It does not apply to the fire, the snake, the oncoming truck — System 1 earns its salary there. "Sleep on it" is for the other decisions: the letter, the purchase, the promise, the signature. The rule my boss **gave** me, and that I **have followed** for twenty years since, is precise: any decision that is expensive or hard to reverse deserves one night.

One night. Not a year of nights — that is procrastination wearing the costume of wisdom. If the morning answer is the same as the evening answer, act on it *that day*, and notice the quiet confidence that comes from having checked with your best self.

I still have that drawer, by the way. My boss retired in 2015 and it is in my office now. Resignation letters **have gone into** it three times since. The drawer has returned all three, unopened, by morning.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What changed overnight about the author's resignation letter?
2. What are the two reasons sleep improves decisions?
3. When should you NOT "sleep on it" — and what is the opposite trap?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. The author wanted it 40% less the next morning and wanted the job back by Friday — the event was the same but the emotional weather changed.
2. Memory consolidation (drops the heat from the day's hurts) and unconscious processing (the sleeping brain tests arguments without ego).
3. Not in real emergencies (fire, snake, oncoming truck — System 1's job); the opposite trap is endless postponement disguised as wisdom — if the morning answer matches, act that day.

</details>

## Bài 2 — The Question, Flipped (~380 từ)

A friend of mine has interviewed job candidates for fifteen years, and he **has collected** a small museum of interview questions. His favorite one is not clever at all. It is simply a question asked twice — the second time upside down.

"Tell me why you want this job," he says. The candidate answers, fluently — everyone **has practiced** this direction. Then he leans back and asks: "Now tell me all the reasons this job is *wrong* for you." The room goes quiet. The fluent ones **have been reciting**; now someone has to actually think.

He **has been calling** this "flipping the question", and it is the oldest tool in the thinking toolbox. Stock analysts **have used** it forever: before you buy, write the *bear case* — the strongest argument that you are wrong. Doctors run differential diagnoses: what else could this be? Navy planners run "pre-mortems": imagine the project has already failed — now explain why. Same flip, different uniforms.

Why does it work? Because your brain is a lawyer, not a judge. Ask it for reasons to believe, and it **has been building** that case since you were born. Ask it for reasons to doubt, and it must build a case it has never practiced. The answers arrive slower, rougher — and more honest.

You can flip almost anything this week. "Should I move to Da Nang?" becomes "What would have to be true for staying to be the right call?" — and suddenly the list of things you are *avoiding* rather than choosing appears on the table. "Do I trust this person?" becomes "What would distrust look like, in small daily doses?" — and you notice it has a shape.

The flip is not negativity. My friend does not reject the candidates who answer the flipped question badly because they are pessimists — he rejects them because they **have never argued** with themselves before, and the job is nothing but arguing with yourself, politely, on deadline.

The mind believes what it has been hired to prove. So hire it, once a week, for the other side.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is the interviewer's favorite question, and what happens when he asks it?
2. Give two other examples of "flipping the question" from different professions.
3. Why does the flip work — what is the brain really like, according to the article?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. "Tell me all the reasons this job is *wrong* for you" — the room goes quiet because candidates have only practiced the positive direction.
2. Stock analysts write the bear case; doctors run differential diagnoses; navy planners run pre-mortems (any two).
3. The brain is a lawyer, not a judge — it proves what it is asked to prove; asking for doubt forces a case it has never practiced, so answers are slower and more honest.

</details>

## Bài 3 — Two Doctors, One Diagnosis (~360 từ)

When my mother's heart test came back "unclear" in 2018, the first doctor said one word: surgery. My father, a quiet man, asked the question that **has saved** our family more money and health than any other question in our lives: "Can we hear another opinion?"

The second doctor looked at the same test, asked my mother to walk up two flights of stairs, watched her, and said: "Come back in six months." She **has been coming back** every six months since — seven visits, zero surgeries, one healthy old woman. The first doctor was not evil, and probably not even wrong. He was simply one human, with one set of habits, reading one unclear picture.

We **have been treating** second opinions as an insult to the first expert, and that is exactly backwards. In studies across medicine, second opinions change the diagnosis or the treatment in a striking share of cases. The first expert is not stupid; the first expert is *first*. Everything after them is another sample from the world's noise — and the average of two independent samples **has always beaten** one.

The tool travels far beyond medicine. Before my brother signed the lease on his first shop, he showed the contract to an accountant-friend: two clauses **had been quietly eating** his margin, invisible to anyone who had not seen a hundred leases. Before I accepted my last job, I asked two people who had left that company the one question HR never answers: "What finally made you go?"

There is an art to it, and it is small: ask someone *independent* (no friendship with the first expert, no stake in your choice), ask them the *same raw information* (show the test, the contract — not your summary of it), and ask them *before* you have decided, because a made-up mind **has been rejecting** evidence since minds began.

The first opinion tells you what one trained person saw. The second tells you how much the world disagrees with them. You almost never regret knowing that number.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the second doctor do differently, and what was the outcome?
2. Why are second opinions not an insult to the first expert?
3. What three small rules make a second opinion useful?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. He ran a practical test (climbing stairs) and prescribed "come back in six months" — seven visits since, no surgery, healthy patient.
2. The first expert is not stupid — just *first*; a second independent sample beats a single sample.
3. Ask someone independent, give them the same raw information (not your summary), and ask before your mind is made up.

</details>
