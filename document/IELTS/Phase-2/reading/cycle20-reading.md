## Bài 1 — The Slot Machine in Your Pocket (~390 từ)

The slot machine **is considered** by many designers to be the most perfect interface ever built. Almost no skill is required. The reward arrives in seconds. And the cost per pull is small enough that quitting feels absurd: you are only ever one pull away from the jackpot.

One by one, those exact properties **have been transplanted** into your phone. The pull-lever **was replaced** by the pull-to-refresh gesture — the same wrist movement, the same suspense. The three spinning wheels **were replaced** by a feed whose next card is unknowable until it arrives. Coins **were replaced** by likes, matches, and red badges with numbers on them. In 2008, a developer showed this publicly: slot-machine mechanics **were openly praised** in industry talks as "the pattern that retains users". Nobody needed to be evil; the pattern simply worked, and things that work **get copied**.

There is a moment worth noticing in the casino, and you have felt it on your phone. Researchers who **interviewed** gamblers found that many were most hypnotized not by winning but by the *near-miss* — two cherries and a third that almost landed. The near-miss **is treated** by the brain as a learning signal: *so close — adjust and try again*. Casinos **were built** on that signal. So, now, is your feed: the post that almost went viral, the conversation that almost became a friendship, the one-more-scroll that almost delivered something great. The almost is the hook, because the almost is what tells the brain that success is learnable.

What can honestly be said in defense of the machines? This: they are not enjoyed. Ask yourself how you feel after forty minutes of scrolling — the answer is almost never "happy". That is the difference between liking and wanting that you met in the dopamine article: the loop **is wanted** by your chemistry and **liked** by nobody. A habit you keep and do not enjoy is not a companion; it is a tenant who pays no rent.

The way out **was demonstrated** by the same casinos, interestingly — self-exclusion lists, where gamblers legally ban themselves from the premises in advance. The insight **was borrowed** by a few app users: the app **is deleted** on a Sunday, the account **is logged out** of permanently, the phone **is left** at the door like a coat. Pre-commitment beats momentary courage, because the decision **is made** by the calm version of you and only executed by the weak one.

You cannot out-pull a machine that was tuned for a century. You can decline to sit at it.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What replaced the slot machine's pull-lever, wheels, and coins on your phone?
2. What is a "near-miss", and why does it hook the brain?
3. How did casinos demonstrate the way out, and how is it borrowed by app users?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Pull-to-refresh, the feed's next card, and likes/matches/red badges.
2. Two cherries and a third that almost lands — the brain treats it as a learning signal ("so close, try again"), making success feel learnable.
3. Self-exclusion lists (advance legal bans); app users delete the app, log out permanently, leave the phone at the door — pre-commitment made by the calm self, executed by the weak one.

## Bài 2 — You Are the Product (~370 từ)

There is a sentence **often repeated** in the technology industry, so old it has faded into wallpaper: if you are not paying for the product, you are the product. Like most wallpaper, nobody looks at it anymore. Let us look at it.

Your attention **is bought** and **sold** in auctions that finish in milliseconds, billions of times a day. The buyer is an advertiser; the seller is a platform; the goods are your eyes at a particular moment of mood. Your data is not stolen — it **is traded**, in extraordinary detail, with your nominal consent buried on page forty of a contract that nobody has ever read to the end.

Here is the part that matters for habits. A platform that **is paid** by the minute of your attention is not a neutral tool, like a hammer. It is a machine whose revenue **is directly tied** to how long you remain, and everything about it — the colors, the timing of notifications, the endless scroll, the almost-payoffs — **has been optimized** toward one goal: that you do not leave. When an interface **is designed** around your departure being the failure condition, using it "responsibly" is like drinking responsibly from a firehose.

The engineers who built these systems knew. Several have said so publicly after leaving: notifications **were timed** to arrive at your weakest hours; "streaks" **were invented** to make absence feel like loss; infinite scroll **was adopted** precisely because a natural stopping point — the bottom of the page — **was found** to reduce engagement. The bottom of the page was, literally, removed.

What can one person do? Not rage — arithmetic. Look at where your hours **are actually spent** (screen time settings will show you, honestly and without judgment), and decide whether the trade was worth it. The attention economy **is powered** by unexamined default settings: the phone as first thing in the morning, the feed as the filler of every queue. Defaults **are chosen** by someone else precisely because choosing is work. Take back the work: an afternoon spent re-deciding every default once — notifications off, apps off the home screen, grayscale on — **is repaid** for years.

You are, in one sense, the product. In a better sense, you are the only player at this table who can leave it — and the house knows almost nothing about what happens after you stand up.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What does "you are the product" mean, concretely?
2. Give three design decisions mentioned that keep users from leaving.
3. What is the "arithmetic" response the article suggests?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Your attention is bought and sold in milliseconds auctions — advertisers buy, platforms sell, the goods are your eyes at a particular mood-moment.
2. Notifications timed for weak hours; streaks to make absence feel like loss; infinite scroll removing the natural stopping point.
3. Check actual screen time without judgment, then re-decide every default once — notifications off, apps off the home screen, grayscale on.

</details>

## Bài 3 — The Boring Weekend Experiment (~360 từ)

A friend of mine **was given** an assignment by her therapist, and she swears it changed her life more than a year of self-help books: spend one full weekend with no screens, and do absolutely nothing to fill the gap.

Note the second half, because that is where everyone cheats. Screen-free weekends **are often attempted** with elaborate replacement plans — hikes, brunches, museums, an impressive list of worthy activities. That is not boredom; that is a curated feed of offline content, scheduled by the same anxious machinery. The assignment **was designed** to fail in that exact way, which is why the rule **was written** so plainly: nothing. Sit there. Stare.

Her report, two days later, read like dispatches from an alien planet. The first afternoon **was survived** with difficulty; her hands **kept reaching** for a phone that had been locked in a drawer. By Saturday evening, something unexpected **was noticed**: time had changed texture. Hours felt longer. She **heard** sounds in her apartment that she had never heard — the refrigerator, the street, her own breathing. On Sunday morning, she **started** writing in a notebook — something she had "always wanted to do" for ten years — and the writing **was not planned**; it simply arrived, the way water arrives when a dam is opened.

Boredom, it turns out, **has been slandered** by a century of convenience. The brain in stillness does not idle; it switches to what neuroscientists call the default mode network — the state in which memories **are consolidated**, futures **are simulated**, the self **is interviewed** by itself. Creativity is not manufactured in stimulation; it is *unblocked* in stillness. Every culture knew this until about fifteen years ago: the walk, the bath, the prayer, the fishing line — technologies of nothing, perfected over millennia, **abandoned** in a decade.

The habit lesson is almost embarrassingly practical. The phone **was not removed** from her life after that weekend — it was merely demoted: notifications **were silenced** forever, and the morning **is started** without it, one hour of primitive analog life before the machine wakes up. Her anxiety, she reports, **was cut** in half. Her writing, which had been "wanted" for ten years, **has been kept** for two.

Nothing was added. Something was subtracted. That is often the whole secret.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What was the exact assignment, and how do people cheat on it?
2. What did she notice on Saturday evening and Sunday morning?
3. What is the "default mode network", and what happens there?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. One full weekend with no screens and nothing to fill the gap; people cheat by scheduling elaborate worthy activities — a curated offline feed.
2. Time changed texture, hours felt longer, sounds were heard; unplanned writing simply arrived on Sunday morning.
3. The brain state in stillness — memories are consolidated, futures simulated, the self interviewed by itself; creativity is unblocked there.

</details>
