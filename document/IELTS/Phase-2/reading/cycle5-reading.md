> 3 bài về narrative identity — chủ đề của storytelling workshop.

## Bài 1 — The Author and the Character (~360 từ)

In every person's life, there are two figures: the character and the author.

The character is who things happen TO. The character gets dumped, gets promoted, gets rained on. The character has a childhood, a hometown, a scar on the left knee from a bicycle that is long gone. When the character tells their story, it sounds like weather reports: things occurred, some good, some bad.

The author is who decides what it MEANT. The author looks at the same rain and calls it either "the year everything washed away" or "the year I learned to dance in water." Same rain. Different book.

Here's the trick nobody tells you: you are both — but you spend most of your life thinking you're only the character.

Psychologist Dan McAdams studies what he calls narrative identity: the internal story each of us constructs to make sense of our life. His research found something remarkable. People who tell their lives as contamination stories — good turns bad, joy turns to pain — tend toward depression, regardless of what actually happened to them. And people who tell redemption stories — pain becomes growth, endings become beginnings — tend toward wellbeing, again regardless of what actually happened.

Read that again: regardless of what actually happened. Two people can live the same biography and carry completely different nervous systems, because the author, not the character, decides the genre.

This is not about lying to yourself. A redemption story that says "my childhood was fine" when it wasn't is not redemption — it's denial wearing a costume. The honest redemption story says: it was bad, AND it made something, AND the something is mine.

The character in you will keep collecting weather. That's the character's job. But the author in you holds a pen right now, tonight, this page. Ask: what genre am I writing? If it's a tragedy, ask a harder question: is it a tragedy because of the facts — or because of the pen I've been letting the facts hold?

Take the pen back. The character lives the life. The author decides it was worth living.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What's the difference between the character and the author?
2. What did McAdams find about contamination vs redemption stories?
3. What is an honest redemption story (not denial in costume)?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Character = who things happen TO (weather reports); author = who decides what it MEANT (genre of the book).
2. Contamination storytellers tend toward depression; redemption storytellers toward wellbeing — REGARDLESS of what actually happened.
3. One that says: it was bad, AND it made something, AND the something is mine (not "it was fine").

</details>

## Bài 2 — The Chapter She Closed (~340 từ)

For eleven years, my aunt told one story about her life: "I gave up my career for my family."

It was true. At twenty-nine, she was the youngest department head her company ever had. At thirty, she was pregnant, and her husband's job moved cities, and — this is how the story went — she chose the family. "Gave up." The phrase did all the work in that sentence.

Every time she told it — at dinners, at funerals, to herself at 2 a.m. — the story ended the same way: with a door closing. She was the woman who once almost had something.

Then, two years ago, I was helping her clean out a closet when I found a box of notebooks. She had been writing, all those years — recipes, then short essays, then pages and pages about our family history, interviewing the grandmothers before the grandmothers forgot. Eleven years of quiet work.

"What is this?" I asked.

"Nothing," she said. "Hobby."

But it wasn't nothing. It was a manuscript. She had documented four generations — the war stories, the recipes that existed nowhere else, the letters. A historian friend looked at it and said, honestly: this belongs in the provincial archive.

Here's what struck me most. When the news spread in our family, my aunt started telling a different story. Same facts: left the job at thirty. New sentence: "I chose a life that let me be there when the grandmothers were still remembering." Nothing changed in her past. Everything changed in her grammar.

"Gave up" became "chose." One verb. That's the distance between a wound and a purpose.

She's sixty now. The archive accepted the manuscript last spring. At the small ceremony, someone asked how it felt to be recognized so late. She said: "It's not late. It's chapter two. Chapter one took eleven years to write, and nobody — including me — knew I was writing it."

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What was the old story, and what phrase did all the work?
2. What was in the box of notebooks?
3. How did "one verb" change everything?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. "I gave up my career for my family" — the phrase "gave up" did all the work (door closing).
2. Eleven years of quiet writing: recipes, essays, family history interviews — a manuscript of four generations.
3. "Gave up" became "chose" — the distance between a wound and a purpose (same facts, new grammar).

</details>

## Bài 3 — Looking Back, Kindly (~330 từ)

Here is a small exercise that changed the way I remember my twenties.

Close your eyes and bring back your most embarrassing memory. The presentation you fumbled. The person you liked, and the message you sent at 1 a.m. The haircut in the photo. Feel the cringe arrive — that hot wave that makes your shoulders try to become earrings.

Now, here is the exercise: look at that younger person, from where you stand now. Not the memory — the person. A twenty-two-year-old, standing in the middle of their life with almost no information, no map, no experience, doing the best interpretation of love and ambition available to them at that hour.

Would you say to that person what you say to the memory?

If a stranger told you, "At twenty-two, I sent a risky message because I liked someone and didn't yet know that silence is sometimes an answer" — you would be gentle. Obviously. You might even find it sweet. So why does the same event, wearing your own face, get treated like a crime?

Psychologists call this the hindsight bias, with a side of harshness: because you NOW know how things ended, you judge the person who couldn't have known. You are punishing a beginner for not having the results of an expert. Read that sentence twice — it is almost never spoken aloud, and almost always true.

Your past selves were beginners. Every single one of them. The person who chose the wrong job — beginner at careers. The person who stayed too long — beginner at leaving. They were not stupid. They were standing in fog, holding the small lantern of what they knew, walking anyway.

You are standing in fog right now, by the way. Your forty-year-old self is already looking back at this exact evening, deciding whether to be kind.

Give them instructions. Be kind backwards. It's the only way to teach the future to be kind to you.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is the exercise?
2. What is hindsight bias with a side of harshness?
3. What are all your past selves, according to the text?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Look at the younger PERSON in the embarrassing memory — not the memory — as a beginner with almost no information.
2. Judging the person who couldn't have known by results you only know now (punishing a beginner for lacking expert results).
3. Beginners — standing in fog, holding a small lantern, walking anyway. (And you're in fog right now too.)

</details>
