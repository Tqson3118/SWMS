# 📖 Đọc 1 — The Asch Experiment (~350 từ)

> Bài mở màn tháng 3 — gạch chân mỗi câu Past Perfect (had + V3) bạn tìm thấy.

---

In 1951, a young psychologist named Solomon Asch **had** a question that sounds almost silly today: if everyone around you says a short line is longer than a long line, will you say it too?

He **had** designed the experiment like a trap made of paper. Each "participant" sat in a room with seven others. The task was simple: match one line to another of the same length — answers so obvious that, alone, people got them right about 99% of the time. But the seven "others" were not participants at all. They were actors, and they **had** been instructed to give the same wrong answer, with calm, boring confidence.

By the time the real participant gave his twelfth answer, the room **had** agreed, loudly and wrongly, eleven times. A third of all responses conformed to the group. Seventy-five percent of participants **denied** their own eyes at least once. Afterwards, they **had** explanations ready: maybe I misunderstood the task; maybe the group knew something I didn't; maybe it was easier to be wrong with everyone than right alone.

But here is the part people forget. Asch **had** also built a second version of the experiment, with one small change: a single dissenter — one actor who answered correctly before the participant spoke. Conformity **dropped** by roughly three quarters. One voice, one honest stranger, was enough to break the spell for almost everyone.

Think about what that means. The pressure that bends us is strong, but it is not made of steel — it is made of *unanimity*. One visible person who says "I see it differently" **has** freed more minds than a thousand silent doubts.

You have met Asch's room many times: the family dinner where everyone laughs at the same target, the meeting where the plan is clearly wrong and everyone nods, the group chat where one joke goes too far and nobody types "stop". In each of those rooms, the math is waiting: unanimity breaks the moment one person speaks.

The question the experiment leaves in your hand is not "would I conform?" — Asch **had** already shown that almost everyone does, whatever they predict. The question is simpler and harder: *when the eleventh wrong answer comes, in which room will you be the one honest stranger?*

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What was the design of Asch's experiment, and what happened to 75% of participants?
2. What was the "one small change" in the second version, and what effect did it have?
3. According to the article, what exactly is social pressure "made of"?
