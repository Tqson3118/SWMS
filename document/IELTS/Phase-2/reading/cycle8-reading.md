## Bài 1 — The Question That Decides (~380 từ)

My father has sold furniture for thirty years, and he **has learned** one lesson better than any psychologist I **have read**: the answer is decided by the question.

A couple walks in. My father does not ask "do you want this table?" He asks: "Are you the kind of family that eats together?" Nobody in the history of retail **has ever answered** no to that question. In ten minutes they **have been measuring** their kitchen for a table "that fits the family we are."

Psychologists call this **framing**, and they **have been studying** it since the 1980s. Same meat, two labels: "75% lean" versus "25% fat". Same surgery, two sentences: "90% survive" versus "10% die". People choose the first version — of the meat, and of the surgery — in huge numbers. Nothing has changed except the frame around the picture.

Frames **have not come** to trick us from outside; we build them ourselves, daily. "Should I quit my job?" is a frame. "What would I do with ten free hours a week?" is a different frame around the same life. The first question **has already decided** that quitting is the only move; the second has not decided anything yet — it **has just opened** a door.

Last month my cousin **called** me about her boyfriend. "Should I break up with him?" she asked. For an hour I listened and said almost nothing, because I **have learned** what my father knows: whoever frames the question holds the steering wheel.

Finally I asked: "What do you want your life to look like at forty?" Silence. Then: "Wow. That's... a different question." She **has been thinking** about the forty-year-old version of herself since that night — and, she told me last week, the boyfriend question **has quietly answered** itself.

The frame is not the picture. But most people never look at the frame — they only look through it. Once you have seen a frame, you cannot fully un-see it. That **has been** the gift of this week's reading: not new eyes, just the knowledge that eyes have edges.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What trick does the father use to sell tables?
2. What is the "75% lean vs 25% fat" example about?
3. What happened when the author reframed the cousin's question?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. He asks a question nobody refuses ("Are you the kind of family that eats together?") — the question decides the answer.
2. Identical information framed two ways changes people's choices (framing effect).
3. She stopped asking "should I break up?" and started thinking about the life she wants at forty — and the original question answered itself.

</details>

## Bài 2 — The Skeptic's Trap (~400 từ)

Uncle Phong is the smartest person I know, and that is exactly his problem.

He **has read** more books than anyone in the family. He **has spotted** every scam, every lie, every trick — in other people. But since 2015, he **has believed** one investment scheme with his whole heart. Every month the scheme "pays" a small profit; every month his belief gets a little stronger. When my mother asked him why he never checks the company's license, he said the most human sentence I **have ever heard**: "You just don't want me to succeed."

That sentence is the sound of **confirmation bias** closing its door.

Here is how the trap works. You hold a belief — any belief. Your brain, a loyal employee, goes out and collects evidence for it. It **has been doing** this job for your whole life, silently, and it is very good at the work. Supporting facts get a warm welcome at the front door. Opposing facts knock, and the brain checks the list of reasons to ignore them: *that source is biased, that sample is small, they don't understand the situation.* The door stays shut. Nobody in there feels dishonest — the security guard **has already left** for the night.

The scariest finding in the research is not that we fool ourselves. It is that intelligence makes it worse. Smarter people **have not proven** better at escaping the trap; they **have proven** better at defending it. Uncle Phong's library gives him a bigger vocabulary for "why they are wrong".

But there is a door out, and it is embarrassingly small. Before an important belief, write down: *"This belief is wrong if ______."* Fill in the blank in advance — before the evidence arrives, before the guard goes home. Kahneman's generation **has called** this kind of thinking by many names; the plainest is *being fair to the other side of your own argument*.

I said that to Uncle Phong once. He listened, nodded slowly, and said I had been reading too many books. He **has not checked** the license yet — but last week, for the first time, he asked me what the company's tax code was.

Small door. But it **has opened**.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What has Uncle Phong believed since 2015, and how does he react to questions about it?
2. Why does intelligence make confirmation bias worse, according to the article?
3. What is the practical way out that the article suggests?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. An investment scheme; he treats questions as envy ("You just don't want me to succeed").
2. Smart people are better at defending the trap — a bigger vocabulary for "why they are wrong" — not better at escaping it.
3. Write "This belief is wrong if ______" in advance — define disconfirming evidence before it arrives.

</details>

## Bài 3 — The Halo (~360 từ)

First impressions **have decided** more jobs, marriages, and prison sentences than any of us would like to admit.

Psychologists **have named** the effect after the painter's trick: the halo — a circle of light above a saint's head. Put one positive glow on a person, and the mind lights everything else about them with the same warm circle. Tall people seem more leader-like. Attractive defendants **have received** lighter sentences, decade after decade, in study after study. One well-written sentence at the top of a CV **has been boosting** interview scores for generations.

I met my halo at university. The first week, a classmate answered one question brilliantly in a seminar — one question — and for two years I **believed** everything she said. She quoted studies that did not exist, and my brain nodded along, comfortable in the light. When she finally said something badly wrong in front of the whole class, I felt almost betrayed — as if a lamp had lied to me about being the sun.

The halo does not only work on others; it works on you, from you. You buy the expensive pen because one advertisement **has attached** "successful person" to it — and you carry that pen into meetings like a small lamp. Teachers **have been grading** neat handwriting as intelligence for a century. You **have done** it too: the colleague with the confident voice "must know what she is talking about".

What can you do? Almost nothing, and one thing. The almost-nothing: you cannot stop the halo from appearing — it is System 1's wallpaper. The one thing: you can separate *qualities*. When you catch yourself trusting someone, pause and list what you actually know about them: skill A, experience B, charm C. Keep the list. Charm is real; it is simply not the same item as skill.

A halo is beautiful. It is just not a flashlight. It lights the person you want to see — **not** the person in front of you.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is the "halo effect", and where does its name come from?
2. What happened with the brilliant classmate at university?
3. What is the one practical defense the article offers?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. One positive quality lights up everything else about a person in our eyes; named after the circle of light painted above saints' heads.
2. The author trusted everything she said for two years (including fake studies) — until she was publicly wrong and the illusion broke.
3. Separate qualities: list what you actually know (skill, experience, charm) and treat charm as real but different from skill.

</details>
