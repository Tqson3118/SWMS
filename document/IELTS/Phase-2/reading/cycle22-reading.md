## Bài 1 — The Muscle That Wasn't (~400 từ)

For about twenty years, one of the most famous ideas in psychology was the "muscle" theory of willpower. In the classic experiments, subjects **were asked** to resist fresh cookies while eating radishes, and afterwards they gave up faster on a puzzle. Willpower, it **was concluded**, **is depleted** by use, like a battery — and the theory **was given** a name that stuck: ego depletion. An entire self-help industry **was built** on it: willpower is limited, so spend it carefully, and refill it with glucose.

Then the science did what science occasionally does: it checked. Large replication projects **were run** across many labs. The effect shrank. Some studies found nothing; a few found the opposite. The batteried self **was quietly retired** from the textbooks, and something more interesting **was left standing** in its place.

The rescue came from a detail in the original studies. People gave up faster on the puzzle *if they believed* willpower was limited. Volunteers who **were told** that mental effort energizes them — that the battery recharges with use — performed *better* after a hard task. In another famous study, people **were observed** during their working day: those who felt their work **was chosen by them** showed little depletion, while those who felt pushed around by others collapsed by afternoon. It is the same lesson the two rats taught you about stress: control and belief **are fed** into the same meter as energy.

A third stream of research **was contributed** by the personality people: self-control, measured over years, turned out to be much less about resisting temptation and much more about *avoiding* it. The students with the best self-control, when **were given** pagers and beeped at random moments, reported facing *fewer* temptations — not winning more battles. Their lives **were arranged** so the war rarely started: no snacks in the house, no phone in the pocket, no decision left to the weak hour.

Put the three findings together and the practical picture **has been redrawn** completely. Willpower is real, but it is less like a battery and more like an emotion — a signal that rises when something matters, influenced by belief, mood, and whether the situation feels chosen. And the person with "great self-control" is usually not a warrior at all. They are an engineer, standing in a well-designed room where almost nothing needs resisting.

This is the best news a tired person can receive. You do not need a bigger battery. You need a smaller number of battles — and a mind that believes effort feeds itself, doing work that feels, at least partly, like its own choice.

The muscle that wasn't. The engineer who never fights. The war that never starts.

2. What did the pager study find about people with high self-control?
3. According to the article, what is the practical picture that has been redrawn?

2. They reported facing FEWER temptations — their lives were arranged so the war rarely started.

## Bài 2 — The Architecture of the Easy Thing (~370 từ)

Every behavior has a cost of entry, and designers have a word for it: friction. Sending an email is low friction; driving to the gym is high friction. The insight of the last decade of habit science can be compressed into one sentence: **friction is destiny** — mostly because whoever sets the friction sets your future.

The proof **was collected** in dozens of natural experiments. When a cafeteria **was rearranged** so water was placed at eye level and soda behind the counter, water consumption rose measurably — no signs, no lectures, no willpower. When companies **made** retirement enrollment automatic, with an opt-out instead of an opt-in, savings rates jumped by multiples; the identical employees with identical salaries had simply **been handed** a different default. Organ donation rates across Europe **are divided** not by culture or kindness but by the form: countries where you are checked "donor" unless you say no have roughly double the rates of countries where you must tick the box yourself.

You have met this force already under its commercial name. Apps **are engineered** toward zero friction: one tap to order, one thumb-flick to refresh, payment details **stored** forever so the wallet never intervenes. The engineers did not invent the psychology; they merely aim it. Your best interest **is not consulted** in their metrics. The friction that was removed from the purchase **was added**, silently, to your decade.

The practical tool is symmetrical, and it is called choice architecture. To build a good habit: *reduce the friction until refusal is awkward*. Running shoes **are placed** by the door, worn before the coffee is poured; the book **is put** on the pillow; the guitar **is left** on a stand, not in a case — because a case, it turns out, is where guitars go to be admired. To break a bad one: *raise the friction until the behavior is annoying*. The phone **is moved** to another room at night — twenty steps of friction has killed more doomscrolling than a thousand resolutions; the app **is logged out** of after every use; the snacks **are not bought** in the first place, because no one has ever eaten a snack that was not in the house as often as one that was.

The rule of thumb is almost poetic: *make the good thing stupid-easy and the bad thing stupid-annoying*. Character gets the credit; architecture does the work. Design the room tonight, and tomorrow morning you will wake up in a place where the right thing is simply the path of least resistance.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What did the cafeteria and retirement experiments show?
2. What is "choice architecture", and how is it applied to building vs. breaking habits?
3. Give the rule of thumb at the end, in your own words.

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Moving water to eye level raised water consumption; automatic enrollment multiplied savings — defaults, not persuasion, change behavior.
2. Designing the environment: reduce friction for good habits (shoes by the door, book on the pillow) and add friction for bad ones (phone in another room, apps logged out).
3. Make the good thing stupid-easy and the bad thing stupid-annoying.

</details>

## Bài 3 — The Smallest Possible Yes (~350 từ)

A Stanford researcher once **was asked** why so many of his habit-change patients failed, and his answer became a method: they were trying to do too much. His counter-proposal **was called** the Tiny Habit: make the new behavior so small that it cannot fail. Not "floss your teeth" — floss ONE tooth. Not "meditate twenty minutes" — take three breaths after parking the car. Not "write a page" — open the document and write one sentence.

The psychology has two floors. The obvious one: a tiny behavior requires no motivation, so it happens even on your worst day — and a habit that survives your worst day is a real habit, not a fair-weather friend. The less obvious one: success, however small, **is celebrated** immediately — a fist pump, a whispered "victory" — because the brain learns from *feelings*, not from intentions. The emotional moment after the behavior is where the loop **is actually wired**. Most self-improvement programs **are designed** around the size of the effort and ignore the size of the feeling; the recipe, in reality, runs the other way.

A second tool **was popularized** by research on implementation intentions — a technical name for a humble sentence: "When X happens, I will do Y." The cue **is written** in advance and attached to an existing routine, a method known as habit stacking: "After I pour my coffee, I will review three flashcards." Studies found that people who **were given** a specific when-where plan followed through at roughly double the rate of people who held merely an intention — the same goal, the same motivation, the same person, but a sentence with a hinge in it.

Why does the tiny yes work so unreasonably well? Because identity **is built** from evidence, not from declarations. Each flossed tooth **is recorded** by the brain as a vote: *I am the kind of person who flosses*. The votes accumulate; one day the behavior scales itself, because the person has changed and the person wants more. You do not rise to the level of your goals, and you do not fall to the level of your excuses — you settle, gently and daily, at the level of your smallest yes.

So this is the week to shrink something. Take the habit you have failed at three times, cut it by ninety percent, attach it to something you already do, and celebrate it like a fool when it happens. Floss one tooth. Then let the tooth make the argument.

2. "When X happens, I will do Y" attached to an existing routine; roughly double the follow-through rate.
