## Bài 1 — The Ticket You Cannot Refund (~400 từ)

My aunt **has been keeping** a broken piano in her living room for eleven years.

It cost her a fortune in 2013. The seller promised it would hold its value; it has not. Moving it downstairs would cost more than the piano is worth. Selling it would bring, maybe, one-fifth of what she paid. So it stands there, in the middle of the room, like a polite guest who refuses to leave — and every six months, she **has been paying** a tuner to keep it "alive".

Economists **have been arguing** for decades about what to call this. Their coldest name is the **sunk cost fallacy**: money already spent is gone, no matter what you do next, so it should have no vote in tomorrow's decisions. If the piano gives no joy, the rational move is to let it go — today, not after one more tuning.

But the fallacy is not really about money. It is about stories. My aunt is not protecting a piano; she is protecting the younger woman who bought it, the one who dreamed of Sunday concerts with the neighbors. Closing the piano means agreeing that the dream **has failed** — and admitting failure costs more pride than the tuner charges.

You **have been paying** your own tuners, quietly. The degree you finished and hate — so all doors must stay somehow "open". The friendship that **has been dying** for three years, kept on machines because you two "have history". The project at work nobody believes in anymore, defended in meetings because of the months already buried in it.

Here is the question that cuts through it. Not *"what have I already spent?"* — that money, that time, that love is already in the ground. The question is: **"If I were starting today, knowing what I know, would I choose this again?"**

If the answer is no — and it usually is, once the question is asked honestly — then every extra day is not loyalty. It is a new cost, paid today, to protect a decision made yesterday by a person who knew less than you do now.

My aunt sold the piano last spring. The living room **has felt** bigger since, she says. She **has been using** the empty space for something new: nothing yet. Just space. Apparently, that is what letting go buys you — not money, but room.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. Why does the aunt keep paying the tuner, according to the deeper explanation?
2. What is the "cutting question" that beats the sunk cost fallacy?
3. What did selling the piano actually buy her?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Not the piano — she is protecting the younger self who dreamed; closing it means admitting the dream failed.
2. "If I were starting today, knowing what I know, would I choose this again?"
3. Not money, but room/space — the freedom to start something new.

</details>

## Bài 2 — "I Knew It All Along" (~390 từ)

Every January, my friends and I **have been making** the same bet for ten years now: each of us predicts the year — stock market up or down, our football team's final rank, who in the group will get married first. We write the predictions on paper, sign them, and lock them in a drawer.

You already know what happens when we open the drawer in December. Every single person **has been telling** the story of the year as if the paper were a formality. "Of course the market fell — anyone could see the interest rates." "I always said those two would marry." The papers say something else. The papers always say something else.

Psychologists **have been calling** this **hindsight bias** since the 1970s: once the outcome is known, the mind quietly rewrites history so that it "always knew". The effect is so reliable that doctors **have been misremembering** their own earlier diagnoses in studies — not lying, genuinely remembering themselves as more right than they were.

Why does the brain do this? Because "I knew it" feels safe. A predictable world is a manageable world. If the disaster was visible in advance, then next time I can see it coming too. The price of this comfort is brutal: if you **have been telling** yourself that everything was predictable, you never learn which predictions were actually skill and which were luck.

And that confusion — skill versus luck — is the expensive one. Investors who **have been crediting** themselves for bull markets take bigger risks with borrowed money. Managers who "saw the product's success from day one" stop listening to the quiet analysts who did not.

The drawer is my insurance. When December comes, the paper forces the December-me to meet the January-me: one of them knew, one of them guessed, and the difference is written in ink. Some years I have been embarrassingly wrong. Those are the useful years — the only ones that **have been teaching** me where my judgment ends and the world's chaos begins.

The past looks inevitable from the inside. It never was. Say it slowly, because your memory disagrees: *it never was.*

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What happens every December when the friends open the drawer of predictions?
2. Why does the brain rewrite history ("I knew it all along")?
3. What is the "expensive" confusion that hindsight bias causes?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Everyone retells the year as if their correct answers were obvious — even though the papers show otherwise.
2. Because a predictable world feels safe/manageable ("next time I can see it coming too").
3. Confusing skill with luck — e.g., investors crediting themselves for bull markets and taking bigger risks; managers stopping listening to analysts.

</details>

## Bài 3 — Why Sharks Sell Newspapers (~370 từ)

Question: are shark attacks more common in years when plane crashes are in the news, or less common?

Neither — shark attacks have nothing to do with plane crashes. But people judge them together, because the brain sells them on the same shelf. Whatever is easy to picture feels likely; whatever takes statistics to see, feels rare. Psychologists **have been calling** this the **availability bias** since the 1970s, and it **has been running** your news feed longer than you have been reading one.

The mechanism is simple. Memory is not a library; it is a stage. Whatever stands in the spotlight feels important — and vivid, emotional, recent stories push everything else into the dark. One airplane disaster **has been dominating** a thousand safe landings in every mind since newspapers began. My grandmother feared planes her whole life and smoked two packs a day: the crash she could picture; her lungs, she could not.

I caught the bias in myself last summer. For three weeks I **had been** refusing to let my son swim in the sea — a video of a jellyfish swarm had gone everywhere. Meanwhile, in those same three weeks, my neighbor's son broke his arm on the trampoline we **had been letting** our kids jump on all year. The numbers were not on my side; the pictures were.

The defense is not "stop feeling". The defense is asking the boring question the news never asks: *compared to what?* Before the fear decides, look for the base rate — how often does the thing actually happen, to people like you, in a year? Sharks kill about ten people a year, worldwide. Cows kill more. Bees kill more. The drive to the beach — do not start me on the drive to the beach.

Vividness is not evidence. It is only volume. The loudest story in your head **has been winning** arguments it never earned, all your life — and it will keep winning, unless you ask it, politely, for the numbers.

**Câu hỏi:**
1. What is the availability bias, in your own words?
2. What two examples does the author give of vivid fear beating real statistics?
3. What is the "boring question" that defends against this bias?

<details><summary>ĐÁP ÁN (nhấn để mở)</summary>

1. Judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind — vivid, emotional, recent stories feel more probable than they are.
2. Grandmother fearing planes while smoking two packs a day; the author banning sea swimming over a jellyfish video while ignoring the trampoline injuries.
3. "Compared to what?" — look for the base rate: how often does it actually happen to people like you?

</details>
