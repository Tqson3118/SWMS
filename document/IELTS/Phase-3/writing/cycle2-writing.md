# Writing — Cycle 2: descriptions that stay accountable

This three-day writing cycle practises the non-defining relative clauses from Grammar 2 with fictional teams and observable actions. Every task asks for a 200–250 word response. Spend 50 minutes: understand the brief and plan (8), draft (15), check meaning and commas (10), revise (10), and self-score (7). The models are original course writing, not mandatory answers. A different well-supported response can meet the same criteria.

## Cách học và tự chấm

Dành 50 phút mỗi ngày: đọc yêu cầu và lập dàn ý (8), viết bản nháp (15), kiểm tra nghĩa và dấu phẩy (10), sửa bài (10), tự chấm (7). Viết 200–250 từ trước khi mở bài mẫu. Gạch chân mệnh đề quan hệ, chỉ ra danh từ được bổ nghĩa và thử bỏ phần thông tin thêm để kiểm tra dấu phẩy. Với mỗi tiêu chí trong bảng chấm: 2 điểm nếu đáp ứng đầy đủ và chính xác, 1 nếu đáp ứng một phần hoặc còn lỗi nhỏ, 0 nếu thiếu hoặc sai nghĩa. Tổng 12 điểm chỉ dùng để chọn mục cần luyện tiếp. Giữ bản nháp, bản sửa và một câu giải thích sự thay đổi; bài mẫu không phải đáp án duy nhất.

## D5 — A recommendation email about a library event

### Task

Write a 200–250 word email to Lan, the fictional reading-club organiser. Recommend how the club should assign Minh and the organiser Lan, who also volunteers, to a children’s event. Use one defining relative clause to identify a volunteer or resource, one non-defining clause with correct commas, and one **whose** clause. Describe two observable actions under different conditions, explain one practical recommendation, and state one limitation about what the observations cannot prove. Do not diagnose or assign a permanent personality type.

### Process and self-check

1. Plan two evidence points: what happened, and where or when it happened.
2. Draft the recommendation before choosing labels; underline each relative clause.
3. Revise: ask what each clause modifies, test whether commas change the meaning, and replace any prediction about future behaviour with a bounded statement.

**Visible optional hints:** (1) use a defining clause to identify “the volunteer ___ prepared the map”; (2) use commas around extra information about a named person or place; (3) put **whose** immediately before its noun, as in “a volunteer whose notes…”.

**Transfer and self-score:** After revising, write one sentence explaining which evidence most supports your recommendation. Score 0–2 each for task fulfilment, evidence and limitation, defining/non-defining meaning, **whose** grammar and comma control, organisation, and revision quality (12 points). Note one acceptable alternative sentence you could have written.

<details><summary>Original model, annotations, and full criteria</summary>

### Original model (211 words)

Dear Lan,

I recommend that Minh and Lan work together at Saturday’s children’s event. Minh is the volunteer who prepared a map of the reading stations, so he can explain the route that children will follow. Lan, who has organised the club’s previous events, can welcome families and adjust the order when a queue changes. Minh’s map, whose arrows show two possible routes, gives the team a useful starting point, but it does not show how children will respond in a crowded room.

During the quiet planning session, Minh explained each station clearly and answered questions after he had time to prepare. During the library’s busy afternoon, he waited beside the books while Lan spoke with visitors, then suggested a shorter route when several children arrived together. These observations support a practical division of work: Minh can explain the prepared activity, while Lan can notice changes and ask for a quick decision.

I would still give both volunteers a short briefing before the event. One afternoon does not prove that Minh always needs preparation or that Lan will solve every problem. The team should record what happens and review the plan afterwards. That evidence will help the club decide which arrangement to repeat and which part to change.

Best wishes,

Maya

### Annotation and worked revision

- **who prepared a map** identifies which volunteer; **that children will follow** identifies which route. They are defining, so no commas.
- **who has organised…** adds information about the named Lan, so commas are required. **whose arrows** links the map to its arrows; it does not label Minh.
- A weak draft said, “Minh is independent and Lan is sociable.” The revision replaces labels with the quiet-planning and busy-afternoon evidence, then states the limitation.

### Rubric and acceptable alternatives (12 points)

- 0–2 task coverage: 200–250 words, email purpose, recommendation, and limitation.
- 0–2 evidence: two concrete actions tied to different conditions.
- 0–2 meaning: defining clauses identify nouns; non-defining commas add information.
- 0–2 grammar: accurate **whose** + noun and relative-clause punctuation.
- 0–2 organisation: clear recommendation, evidence, action, and limitation.
- 0–2 revision: explains a change and removes unsupported prediction.

Accept “the volunteer who checked the list” or “the list the volunteer checked” when the roles are clear. Accept any practical division supported by two observations. A response needs revision before scoring if it treats a single observation as a permanent personality fact.

## D6 — A process note for a repair café

### Task

Write a 200–250 word process note for Sara, who manages a fictional repair café. Explain how the café should record a busy shift so that later decisions are fair. Use one defining relative clause, one non-defining **which** clause referring to a named object or whole preceding idea, and one formal or natural preposition pattern such as “the table which we worked at” or “the table at which we worked”. Include two observations, one revision to the recording process, and one limitation about cause and effect.

### Process and self-check

1. Plan the note in four parts: situation, evidence, changed process, limitation.
2. Draft one sentence with no commas that identifies a particular tool, shift, or volunteer; draft one comma clause that adds information.
3. Revise the preposition: choose natural final-position **which** or formal **at/in which**, never “in where”; check that **which** has the intended antecedent.

**Visible optional hints:** (1) “the shift that began late” identifies one shift; “the shift, which began late, …” adds information about an already named shift; (2) **which** can refer to a named table or a whole previous idea, but make the reference clear; (3) separate fewer missing tools from a claim that one checklist caused the change.

**Transfer and self-score:** Add one sentence that a later manager could use to repeat or revise the process. Score 0–2 each for purpose and length, evidence, relative-clause meaning, preposition/comma accuracy, fair causal language, and revision quality (12 points). Record one sentence you changed and why.

<details><summary>Original model, annotations, and full criteria</summary>

### Original model (219 words)

Process note for the next repair-café shift

The café should record the conditions that shaped each repair before assigning a description to a volunteer. During Tuesday’s crowded shift, Beth searched for a screwdriver while a visitor waited. The tool tray, which had been moved beside the payment box, contained several unfamiliar parts. Beth then made a checklist that two new volunteers used during the next shift. They found the tray quickly, although one cable was still missing.

Arun, who usually arrives with a labelled bag, stayed late after his regular job and forgot to return a borrowed cable. This example should remain in the record because it complicates the first description of him as organised. The note should state what happened, where the equipment was, and how much time each person had. At the table at which we worked, the volunteers also agreed to mark borrowed items before visitors left.

For the next three weeks, record the checklist, the shift’s busyness, missing tools, and any change in the storage system. Review the notes with Beth and Arun, who can correct an incomplete account. If fewer tools go missing, the café may report that fewer tools were missing during that period. It should not claim that the checklist alone caused the change, because schedules, visitors, and equipment may also change.

### Annotation and worked revision

- **that shaped each repair** identifies the conditions; **which had been moved…** adds information about the named tray and therefore takes commas.
- **at which we worked** is formal and means “where we worked”; “the table which we worked at” is also acceptable. “in where” is not.
- A weak conclusion said, “The checklist prevented losses.” The revision says what was observed and names other changing factors, so it does not claim a controlled causal result.

### Rubric and acceptable alternatives (12 points)

- 0–2 task and length: process note, 200–250 words, and four required parts.
- 0–2 evidence: two concrete observations with conditions or context.
- 0–2 clause meaning: defining versus non-defining information is correctly signalled.
- 0–2 form: **which** antecedents, commas, and preposition pattern are accurate.
- 0–2 causal caution: reports the period without claiming the checklist alone caused it.
- 0–2 revision: identifies a weak claim and shows a clearer replacement.

Accept a natural final preposition or a formal preposition before **which** when the place relation is clear. Accept a different fair recording procedure supported by the observations. Do not require the model’s tool or volunteer names.
</details>

## D7 — A reflective proposal after an unclear assignment

### Task

Write a 200–250 word reflective proposal for a fictional design club after a role assignment created confusion. Explain what the organiser should ask before assigning the next task. Use one defining relative clause, one non-defining clause, and one clause with **whose**. Include a practical proposal, two condition-linked observations, and a closing sentence that limits what the episode proves about the people involved. Use a reflective memo, not an email.

### Process and self-check

1. Plan the contrast: what the organiser assumed, what each person actually did, and what information was missing.
2. Draft the proposal with a clear question for the next meeting; mark defining clauses with D and non-defining clauses with ND.
3. Revise for attachment and commas: named people usually take extra information in commas; a clause that selects one task or person usually does not.

**Visible optional hints:** (1) identify the task that needs a particular skill before describing a person; (2) use **whose + noun** for a relationship such as “a designer whose deadline…”; (3) end by limiting the interpretation to this assignment and its conditions.

**Transfer and self-score:** Write one alternative opening that avoids a label and begins with an observable action. Score 0–2 each for proposal and length, evidence, clause meaning, **whose** and comma control, practical clarity, and thoughtful limitation/revision (12 points). Keep both openings and explain which is clearer.

<details><summary>Original model, annotations, and full criteria</summary>

### Original model (233 words)

Reflective proposal: planning the next exhibition task

The organiser should begin by asking what each job requires and what information each member has. In the previous assignment, Rosa was given the poster because she was called independent. Daniel was asked to rewrite another student’s presentation because he was called cooperative. The poster, which appeared on the college website, needed a group decision about its message. The presentation required subject knowledge and more time than Daniel had before an examination.

Daniel carried display boards and checked the labels on the morning of the exhibition, but he declined the rewriting request. His decision was connected to the deadline and to his limited knowledge of the topic. Rosa worked on the visual design, and two members checked the wording with her. These examples do not show that Daniel refuses help or that Rosa always works alone. They show that a role can combine a person’s preference with the conditions of the task.

For the next project, the club should list the decisions that a role includes, the time available, and the person whose deadline might affect the plan. Members can then choose a task that matches their information and negotiate support before work begins. The organiser should hold a short check-in, which will reveal whether the assignment is still workable. This episode offers a question for the next meeting, not a permanent category for either member.

### Annotation and worked revision

- **which appeared on the college website** is non-defining because the poster is already identified; commas add information. **whose deadline** links a person with a deadline.
- **because she was called independent** reports the organiser’s assumption; the model then replaces it with task requirements and observable actions.
- A weak opening said, “Rosa is independent, but Daniel is cooperative.” The revised opening asks what each job requires and what information each member has, which makes the proposal actionable.

### Rubric and acceptable alternatives (12 points)

- 0–2 task and length: reflective proposal, 200–250 words, and clear next step.
- 0–2 evidence: two observations tied to assignment conditions.
- 0–2 meaning: defining and non-defining clauses serve different purposes.
- 0–2 form: **whose** + noun, commas, and antecedents are accurate.
- 0–2 practical proposal: asks about requirements, time, decisions, or support.
- 0–2 limitation and revision: avoids a permanent category and explains a changed opening.

Accept a different memo structure or reasonable task proposal when it uses the passage’s evidence logic. Accept **whose** for a person or object relationship when the noun after it is clear. A response must revise any sentence that treats one role assignment as proof of a fixed trait.
</details>
