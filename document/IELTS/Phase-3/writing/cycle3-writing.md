# Writing — Cycle 3: place, time, reason, and association

This cycle practises **where**, **when**, **why**, and **whose** through fictional descriptions of teams and study settings. Every task requires a 200–250 word response. Spend 50 minutes: interpret the brief and plan (8), draft (15), check antecedents and punctuation (10), revise (10), and self-score (7). The models are original course writing and are examples of reasoning, not mandatory wording.

## Cách học và tự chấm

Dành 50 phút mỗi ngày: đọc yêu cầu và lập dàn ý (8), viết bản nháp (15), kiểm tra danh từ được bổ nghĩa và dấu câu (10), sửa bài (10), tự chấm (7). Viết 200–250 từ trước khi mở bài mẫu. Chỉ gọi **where/when/why** là mệnh đề quan hệ khi nó bổ nghĩa cho danh từ chỉ nơi chốn, thời gian hoặc lý do; một mệnh đề **when** chỉ hoàn cảnh của hành động có thể là mệnh đề trạng ngữ. Với mỗi tiêu chí: 2 điểm nếu đáp ứng đầy đủ và chính xác, 1 nếu chỉ đáp ứng một phần hoặc còn lỗi nhỏ, 0 nếu thiếu hoặc sai nghĩa. Tổng 12 điểm dùng để chọn phần cần luyện lại, không chứng nhận trình độ. Giữ bản nháp và bản sửa, rồi giải thích ít nhất một thay đổi.

## D9 — An observation memo about two study settings

### Task

Write a 200–250 word observation memo for a fictional study coordinator comparing how one learner works in two settings. Include one **where** clause for a place, one **when** clause for a time, and one **whose + noun** clause expressing association. Explain two observable differences, give one practical suggestion, and state why the observations do not prove a fixed personality description. Use clear defining or non-defining commas.

### Process and self-check

1. Plan the two settings, the actions observed, and the suggestion that follows from them.
2. Draft one sentence with **where**, one with **when**, and one with **whose + noun**; underline the noun each clause relates to.
3. Revise: replace **where** with *in which* once to test the place meaning, and replace **when** with *during which* to test the time meaning. Check commas and remove any diagnosis or forecast.

**Visible progressive hints:** (1) choose a room for **where** and a session/day for **when**; (2) put a noun after **whose**, such as “whose notes”; (3) end with a sentence limited to this observation and these settings.

**Transfer and self-score:** Write one alternative sentence using *in which* or *during which*. Score 0–2 each for task/length, evidence, **where/when** meaning, **whose** form and punctuation, practical suggestion, and limitation/revision (12 points). Record one antecedent you checked.

<details><summary>Original model, annotations, and full criteria</summary>

### Original model (227 words)

Observation memo: Minh’s study settings

Minh prepared a reading map in the library where the group kept its quiet tables. He spoke confidently when the task had a clear sequence and he had time to mark the questions first. On the afternoon when the library hosted a children’s event, he moved to a smaller room and wrote his suggestions before joining the discussion. In the room in which he worked, fewer interruptions made it easier for him to compare two possible routes through the text.

Minh’s notebook, whose pages were divided into evidence and questions, helped the group notice what he was responding to. During a later meeting, the room became lively and another learner asked for an immediate decision. Minh did not give the same kind of explanation, but he pointed to the map and asked the group to choose one route. The change is an observation about a task and a setting. It does not prove that Minh is permanently quiet, dependent, or confident.

I suggest giving him the question list before a busy meeting and allowing a short written preparation period. The coordinator should also record what happens in a different room, when the task is less structured, and when another person needs an immediate answer. These notes may help the group plan fairly, but one week of observations cannot explain every future response.

### Annotation and worked revision

- **where the group kept its quiet tables** modifies **library** and expresses place; **when the library hosted…** modifies **afternoon** and is a time relative clause. By contrast, **when the task had a clear sequence…** modifies the circumstances of **spoke** and is an adverbial time clause; there is no time-noun antecedent in that sentence. “The afternoon during which…” is a formal alternative to the relative clause.
- **whose pages** expresses the notebook–pages relationship. It is not *who’s* and must be followed by a noun.
- A weak sentence said, “Minh is introverted.” The revision names the room, task sequence, and preparation time, then limits the conclusion.

### Rubric and acceptable alternatives (12 points)

- 0–2 task and length: memo, 200–250 words, comparison, suggestion, and limit.
- 0–2 evidence: two observable differences tied to settings or times.
- 0–2 **where/when**: each modifies the right place or time noun.
- 0–2 **whose** and punctuation: noun follows **whose** and commas match meaning.
- 0–2 practical suggestion: follows reasonably from the evidence.
- 0–2 revision and boundary: replaces a fixed label with a bounded observation.

Accept *the room in which…* for **where** and *the period during which…* for **when**. Accept any fictional learner and settings if the antecedents are clear. Do not accept **where** after a non-place noun or **whose** without a following noun.

## D10 — A project note about a shared folder

### Task

Write a 200–250 word project note for a fictional team that is revising a shared folder. Explain why one label in the folder should be replaced by a description of actions and conditions. Use two **whose** clauses, one defining and one non-defining, plus one **why** clause that gives a reason. Include a workable process change and a limitation about what the note can establish.

### Process and self-check

1. Plan the old label, two pieces of evidence, the reason the team needs a new description, and the process change.
2. Draft a defining **whose** clause that selects a person or document, then a non-defining **whose** clause with commas for a named item.
3. Revise the **why** clause: it should answer “for what reason?”, not replace a place or time clause. Check that each **whose** is followed by a noun.

**Visible progressive hints:** (1) use “the editor whose checklist…” to identify one editor; (2) use commas around extra information about a named folder; (3) connect the reason to a process, such as recording conditions before interpreting a label.

**Transfer and self-score:** Rewrite the old label in one sentence without any personality adjective. Score 0–2 each for task/length, evidence, defining/non-defining **whose**, reason meaning, process clarity, and limitation/revision (12 points). Note whether each **whose** clause is D or ND.

<details><summary>Original model, annotations, and full criteria</summary>

### Original model (231 words)

Project note: revising the “reliable” folder

The team should replace the label “reliable” with notes about the conditions in which each task was completed. We began with the editor whose checklist included the source date and the person responsible for the next check. She usually finished a review before the meeting, but she requested more time when the shared folder contained unfamiliar files. This evidence suggests a useful process, not a permanent type.

The folder, whose first page lists the current questions, should contain a short record of each revision. Writers can note when a change was requested, where the supporting evidence appears, and why the change was accepted or postponed. The reason why we need this record is that a quick label can hide the difference between a missed deadline, an unclear instruction, and a task that requires outside information.

For the next two weeks, the coordinator should ask the person who changes a file to add one reason and one next action. Another editor should read the note before the meeting, and the team should invite corrections from the writer. If the record becomes more complete, we may say that the process produced fuller notes during that period. We cannot say that the label was a measured personality quality or that the process alone caused every improvement. The folder should help the team ask a fairer question next time.

### Annotation and worked revision

- **the editor whose checklist…** is defining: it selects the editor. **The folder, whose first page…** is non-defining because the folder is already named; commas add information.
- **why we need this record** modifies **reason** and is a reason relative clause. The following **that a quick label can hide…** supplies the content of the reason; it is not another **why** relative. In “note … why the change was accepted”, **why** introduces an embedded question rather than modifying a reason noun.
- A weak sentence said, “The checklist made the editor reliable.” The revision reports fuller notes during a period and names other possible factors.

### Rubric and acceptable alternatives (12 points)

- 0–2 task and length: project note, 200–250 words, old label and process change.
- 0–2 evidence: two conditions or actions, not unsupported adjectives.
- 0–2 defining/non-defining **whose**: selection and extra information are distinguished.
- 0–2 **why**: gives a reason and has a clear antecedent or clause relationship.
- 0–2 process: feasible recording, checking, and correction steps.
- 0–2 limitation and revision: avoids claiming measurement or single-cause effect.

Accept a **why** clause after *the reason* or a natural sentence beginning *This is why…* when the reason is clear. Accept a person or document as the **whose** antecedent. The non-defining version needs commas; the selecting version normally does not.
</details>

## D11 — A formal reflection on a workshop room

### Task

Write a 200–250 word formal reflection after a fictional workshop moved rooms. Explain how place and time affected the group’s work and recommend one change for the next session. Use one **where** clause, one **when** clause, one formal **preposition + whom/which** pattern, and one **whose** clause. Include two evidence points and a final sentence that limits interpretation. Avoid a comma splice and avoid treating a reduced or relative clause as proof of a permanent trait.

### Process and self-check

1. Plan the original room, the new room, the time-linked change, and the recommendation.
2. Draft a formal sentence such as “the colleague to whom we sent the draft” or “the table at which we worked”; identify its preposition relationship.
3. Revise every comma: use a conjunction to coordinate complete ideas, or separate them into sentences. Check that **where** is for place, **when** for time, and **whose** before a noun.

**Visible progressive hints:** (1) choose a real place noun for **where** and a meeting period for **when**; (2) ask “to whom/at which what?” before selecting the formal pattern; (3) split any sentence that joins complete clauses without a conjunction.

**Transfer and self-score:** Convert the formal preposition pattern into a natural final-preposition version and explain the meaning is unchanged. Score 0–2 each for task/length, evidence, **where/when**, formal preposition and **whose**, recommendation, and limitation/revision (12 points). Keep the original and revised sentence.

<details><summary>Original model, annotations, and full criteria</summary>

### Original model (226 words)

Formal reflection on the feedback workshop

The workshop began in the room where the group usually practised short presentations. The tables were wide, and the wall displayed a schedule that everyone could read. On the afternoon when construction started nearby, however, several participants could not hear the recording. We moved to a smaller room in which the shelves reduced the noise, although the narrow tables made it harder to spread out drafts.

The colleague to whom we sent the revised plan suggested a useful compromise. We kept the quiet room for listening and used the library table at which the group could compare sentences during the final discussion. A participant whose notes separated observations from interpretations noticed that the change of room had affected the evidence, not necessarily the group’s interest. Another participant, who had spoken quickly in the first room, wrote a question before speaking in the second.

For future sessions, I recommend reserving the quiet room when a recording is central and scheduling the discussion at a time when construction is unlikely. We should ask participants what they could hear and record which task became difficult. The reflection describes this workshop only. It does not prove that a room determines how anyone participates, or that one schedule will solve every problem. A clear plan should leave space for another observation and a later revision.

### Annotation and worked revision

- **where the group usually practised** modifies **room**; **when construction started** modifies **afternoon**. The formal **in which** and **at which** clauses express place relations.
- **to whom we sent the revised plan** is formal; the natural alternative is “the colleague who we sent the revised plan to”. **Whose notes** places a noun after **whose**.
- A comma plus **and** is valid coordination in “The tables were wide, and the wall displayed…”. A weak draft said, “The room was noisy, we moved.” The revised “The room was noisy, so we moved” supplies the conjunction; two separate sentences would also work. This fixes a real comma splice without treating correct comma-plus-conjunction coordination as an error.

### Rubric and acceptable alternatives (12 points)

- 0–2 task and length: formal reflection, 200–250 words, evidence and recommendation.
- 0–2 evidence: two place/time-linked observations.
- 0–2 **where/when**: accurate place and time relations.
- 0–2 form: formal preposition + **whom/which**, plus **whose + noun**.
- 0–2 recommendation: feasible and connected to the evidence.
- 0–2 limitation and revision: avoids causal/personality overclaim and repairs coordination.

Accept a natural final preposition when meaning is preserved. Accept a different room or schedule if the place/time relation is explicit. Do not mark a sentence wrong merely because it coordinates complete clauses with comma plus **and**.
</details>
