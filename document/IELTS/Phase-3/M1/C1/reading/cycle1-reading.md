# Reading 02 — The notebook that changed the meeting
Bài đọc gốc, tình huống hư cấu. D2–3: 40 phút. Không phải bằng chứng về hiệu quả một can thiệp.

## Passage
The community club has a problem which nobody has described clearly. Every Saturday, members arrive with different expectations. Some want to practise speaking. Others want a quiet hour in which they can read. When the organiser asks whether the session went well, the answers are too general to be useful. “It was fine,” says one person. “It was stressful,” says another. Neither answer tells the organiser what to change.

A volunteer named Amir brings a notebook to the next meeting. Instead of asking members to choose a personality label, he asks three practical questions. What activity did you try? At what point did it become difficult? What would you like to try next time? A member whose answers are normally brief writes that she could not hear her partner near the window. Another member explains that he enjoyed speaking but did not understand the instructions for the second task.

Amir reads the anonymous notes after the session. He does not assume that a quiet person needs a different activity from an outgoing person. He looks for details which can be checked. The problem near the window may be noise. The unclear instructions may need an example. He proposes a small change: start with a written model, then let pairs choose between two places to sit. The organiser agrees to try it for one session rather than announce a permanent solution.

At the following meeting, the club uses the model and asks the same three questions again. Several members report that the first task was easier to understand. However, fewer people attended that day, and the street outside was unusually quiet. Amir writes both details in his notebook. He cannot say that the written example caused all the improvement. The group has changed more than one thing, and the comparison involves different people. The notes are useful for planning even though they are not a controlled experiment.

One member suggests replacing the notebook with a score from one to ten. Amir agrees that a score could make a pattern easier to see, but he wants to keep a short comment as well. A number does not explain whether the difficulty was vocabulary, noise or an unclear instruction. The group decides to use a score alongside one concrete example. Members can leave the form blank if they do not want to take part.

By the end of the month, the notebook contains questions rather than a final verdict. The organiser knows which instructions need a model and which activities members want to repeat. Amir has also learned to write narrower conclusions. Instead of saying, “Everyone prefers this method,” he writes, “Five people who completed the form described the model as helpful.” His sentence is less impressive, but a new volunteer can see exactly what it refers to. That is the kind of record the club can use when deciding what to try next.

## Questions
1. What two specific problems appear in the anonymous notes?
2. What change does Amir suggest, and for how long?
3. Why cannot the club conclude that the model caused all improvement?
4. Why keep a comment beside a score?
5. Who does “who completed the form” refer to in the last paragraph?
6. Rewrite “Amir brings a notebook. The notebook contains questions.” with a defining relative clause.

## Key
1 Noise near the window; unclear instructions for the second task.
2 A written model and a choice of seating, for one session.
3 Attendance and street noise differed, multiple changes occurred, and different people responded.
4 A comment identifies the kind of difficulty; a number alone does not.
5 Five responding people, not everyone in the club.
6 “Amir brings a notebook which/that contains questions.” Accept past tense if both clauses remain consistent.
1 point each, ≥4/6. For Q3 require two passage reasons; reread paragraph4 if incomplete. No outside research knowledge is required.
