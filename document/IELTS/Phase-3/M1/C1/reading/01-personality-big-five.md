# Reading 01 — A description is not a verdict
Bài đọc biên soạn riêng cho C1, không phải nghiên cứu hoặc bản chép nguồn. Mục tiêu: tìm antecedent, phân biệt observation/assumption, trả lời bằng bằng chứng trong bài. 45–55 phút. Đọc lượt đầu không tra từ, lượt hai đánh dấu relative clauses.

## Passage
Maya is a student who has joined a small language club. On her first evening, she sits near the door and speaks only when the organiser asks her a question. Leo, who enjoys meeting new people, decides that she is unfriendly. His friend Sara disagrees. “We have seen her for twenty minutes,” she says. “That is a description of one evening, not a description of her whole life.”

The following week, the club meets in a quiet library. Maya arrives early with a notebook which contains ideas for the next activity. She explains the instructions clearly and helps a member whose phone cannot open the exercise. Leo is surprised. The person whom he thought was uninterested has done more preparation than anyone else. He asks Maya why she said so little at the first meeting. She explains that the café was noisy and that she was tired after work.

During the discussion, the organiser introduces the Big Five as a vocabulary for talking about broad personality dimensions. The five dimensions are openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. They describe degrees rather than five separate types of person. This short introduction does not provide a diagnosis, a hiring tool or an explanation of every action. The members use it to practise English descriptions, while recognising that an informal club observation is not a validated assessment.

Maya says that a word such as “dependable” is useful when it is connected to an example. She describes a colleague who checks whether a shared task is finished before leaving work. Leo describes himself as outgoing because he often starts conversations with new members. Sara asks what happens in other settings. Leo admits that he is much quieter when he has to present to a large audience. He can describe that difference without deciding that one of the descriptions must be false.

The group then rewrites several sentences. “Maya is rude” becomes “Maya did not answer immediately when the café became noisy.” “Leo never listens” becomes “Leo interrupted twice during today's discussion.” These new sentences are less dramatic, but they make it easier to ask a useful question. They also leave room for information which the observer does not yet have. A member can discuss a behaviour without turning it into a permanent judgement about somebody else.

For homework, each student writes about a fictional teammate. The paragraph must include two observations, one possible explanation and one question which could test that explanation. Nobody has to share private experiences. Sara invents a teammate who misses a deadline because the instructions are unclear. She also writes an alternative explanation: the teammate may have underestimated the time required. Her final question is whether the person understood the task before starting it.

At the next meeting, Leo reads his paragraph aloud. He has used “who” for a person, “which” for a notebook and “whose” for someone's plan. More importantly, he has separated what his character did from what he thinks the action means. Maya says that this is the part of the exercise she wants to keep. She is not asking everyone to avoid judgement forever. She is asking them to make a description precise enough that another person can check it, question it or add missing information.

## Tasks
1. Why did Leo's first judgement lack information?
2. Give two details that changed his impression.
3. TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN: Maya dislikes all social activities.
4. TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN: The club used a validated personality assessment.
5. TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN: Leo interrupted twice in the rewritten example.
6. What does “whose” refer to in the second paragraph?
7. Copy one defining and one non-defining relative clause.
8. Explain why Sara gives two explanations, using the final homework instructions.

## Key
1 One short noisy meeting did not show Maya's behaviour elsewhere.
2 Early arrival/prepared notebook, clear explanation, helping with a phone (any two).
3 NOT GIVEN: no statement about all activities.
4 FALSE: explicitly informal, not a validated assessment.
5 TRUE: stated in the rewritten sentence.
6 A member; that member's phone.
7 Defining: “who has joined a small language club”; non-defining: “who enjoys meeting new people” about Leo. Other grammatically valid passage examples accepted.
8 Homework separates observations from interpretations and asks a question to test an explanation; two explanations avoid pretending one is established.
Q1–6: 1 point each; Q7: 2; Q8: 2, total10. ≥7 then review only errors; <7 reread the paragraphs containing the answers.
