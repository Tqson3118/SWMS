# Reading — Cycle 6: cumulative review through changing conditions

This review cycle revisits the relative-clause, possessive-clause, omitted-object, and reduced-clause skills from Cycles 1–5. The three passages are original fiction about people working together. They do not diagnose a person, forecast behaviour, or make claims about CEFR or personality science. Spend 50 minutes per day: preview and glossary (5), first reading (12), four questions (15), evidence check (10), and transfer writing (8). Score each question 0–2 (8 points), then add 0–2 for the transfer’s evidence and grammar, for 10 points per day. Record a first score, use the visible hints, and record a revised score.

## Cách học và tự chấm

Dành khoảng 50 phút mỗi ngày: xem tiêu đề và từ khó (5), đọc lần đầu (12), trả lời bốn câu (15), kiểm tra bằng chứng và gợi ý (10), viết đoạn chuyển giao (8). Làm bài trước khi mở đáp án. Xác định danh từ được bổ nghĩa và vai trò chủ ngữ hoặc tân ngữ; phân biệt mệnh đề quan hệ rút gọn với mệnh đề phân từ chỉ hoàn cảnh. Mỗi câu: 2 điểm nếu đúng ý và giải thích hoặc bằng chứng, 1 nếu đúng một phần, 0 nếu sai hoặc chưa trả lời. Đoạn chuyển giao: 1 điểm cho bằng chứng và giới hạn, 1 cho ngữ pháp và sự gắn kết đúng. Tổng 10 điểm mỗi ngày dùng để tìm phần cần sửa; không chứng nhận trình độ. Ghi điểm lần đầu và sau khi sửa.

## D21 — The rota that needed a reason

### Passage (fictional, 526 words)

The museum’s evening team used a rota that assigned one person to greet visitors and another to check the display cases. The rota worked until a school group arrived with a guide who needed access to a locked cabinet. Mara, who was greeting visitors, left the entrance after telling the next volunteer where she was going. The change helped the guide, but it left the entrance without a named replacement. A visitor waited beside the desk while two volunteers searched for the key.

At the next meeting, the coordinator showed the incident report that Mara had written. The report described the request, the movement of the key, and the time that the entrance had been unattended. It did not describe Mara as careless. Mara said that she had assumed another volunteer would watch the desk because the rota listed the team rather than the person responsible at each moment. Jo, whose job was to revise the rota, agreed that the missing information concerned ownership, not a fixed quality in Mara.

Jo proposed a new column called “next action”. The person who left a station would name the person receiving it, and the person receiving it would repeat the instruction. The rule sounded formal, but the team tested it during a busy afternoon. Mara handed the desk to Arun, who repeated that he would greet the next group. When a guide requested a cabinet key, Arun asked Suki to accompany the guide while he remained at the entrance. The handover took less than a minute.

The team also reviewed a sentence on the visitor card: “Please ask the volunteer needing help.” Jo changed it to “Please ask the volunteer who is available to help.” She explained that the original **-ing** clause was grammatical, but it could suggest a volunteer who was already needing help rather than a volunteer able to assist. The new full relative clause made the intended role clearer. The comma before **and** in the report remained in a sentence that joined two complete ideas; Jo said the conjunction made that coordination clear.

During the following week, the entrance was quiet on some evenings and crowded on others. The new handover was useful in both situations, although the reasons differed. In a quiet period it prevented uncertainty; during a crowd it prevented two people from assuming that the other had taken responsibility. Jo kept the incident report beside the rota because a rule was easier to understand when its reason was visible.

Mara read the final note before signing it. It said that she had responded to a real request with incomplete ownership information, and that the team had changed the system. The note did not erase the unattended entrance, and it did not predict how Mara would act in another museum. It gave the next team a concrete practice: name the receiver, repeat the handover, and record what remains uncertain.

The next rota included a space for the receiver’s initials and a space for a question that still needed checking. Those additions took seconds to complete, yet they allowed the team to revisit an assumption without blaming the person who first noticed the problem.

### Questions

1. What information was missing from the original rota, and how did the new “next action” column address it?
2. Why did Jo prefer “the volunteer who is available to help” to “the volunteer needing help” on the visitor card?
3. In “the incident report that Mara had written” and “the person who left a station”, identify the antecedent and subject/object role of each relative word.
4. What limitation did the final note preserve about interpreting Mara’s action?

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) find who was expected to watch the desk; (2) compare who is helping with who may be needing help; (3) ask who performed **written** and **left**; (4) reread the final two sentences.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Describe a fictional team handover. Include one omitted object relative, one subject relative that remains, and one sentence explaining what an incident report cannot prove. Self-score evidence (0–1) and clause accuracy (0–1).

<details><summary>Hidden key and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. The rota named the team but not the person responsible at each moment. The new column required the person leaving a station to name a receiver and the receiver to repeat the instruction. “It made ownership explicit” is acceptable.
2. “The volunteer needing help” can be read as the volunteer who needs help, while the card intended a volunteer able to help. The full clause makes the intended role explicit. The answer should not say that every **-ing** clause is wrong.
3. **That** refers to **report** and is the object of **had written**, so “the report Mara had written” is possible. **Who** refers to **person** and is the subject of **left**, so it cannot be omitted in the intended structure.
4. It preserved that Mara responded to a real request with incomplete ownership information; it did not erase the unattended entrance or predict how she would behave elsewhere. A response must include both accountability and the limit on generalisation.
</details>

## D22 — The notes beside the window

### Passage (fictional, 502 words)

The reading circle met in a library room whose windows faced a construction site. On quiet mornings, the members discussed a story’s structure around one table. On afternoons when drilling began, they moved to a smaller room where the shelves reduced the noise. The facilitator, An, asked the group to keep notes that separated what a reader noticed from what a reader inferred.

Ben brought a paragraph that described a character who changed plans twice. He called the character unreliable. An asked him to underline the actions that supported the word. Ben marked a cancelled appointment and a later message offering a different time. Linh, who had written the message, explained that the first appointment had overlapped with a family duty. The second change occurred after the other person requested a public place. The details did not remove the inconvenience, but they changed the question from “What type is this character?” to “What conditions shaped these choices?”

The group revised the paragraph. “The character, who cancelled the first meeting, sent a new message,” Ben wrote. An asked whether the commas were correct. They were: the clause added information about a named character rather than identifying which character. Ben then wrote, “The message that Linh received explained the new time.” Here **that** identified the message and acted as the object of **received**. The group compared the two sentences to see how punctuation affected meaning.

During the next meeting, Linh brought a chart whose headings had been copied from an earlier project. One heading said “calm readers”. Linh replaced it with “readers pausing before a reply”. An asked whether the reduced phrase described a stable trait. Linh said it described an observable action in the meeting. A member who preferred quick discussion could pause before replying; a member who usually wrote first could also do so. The phrase named an event, not an inner explanation.

The group then checked a sentence that began, “Reviewing the difficult paragraph, An asked for one concrete example.” An explained that this opening was an adverbial participle clause. It described the circumstance in which An asked the question; it was not a reduced relative clause modifying An. The subject understood from the opening was An, so the attachment was logical. Another sentence read, “The examples selected by the group were placed beside the chart.” That reduced passive clause modified **examples**, which received the selecting.

Before leaving, the members wrote a short rule for future meetings: record the action, the condition, and the interpretation separately. The construction noise had changed the room, but it had not changed every reader in the same way. The notes beside the window therefore contained both a useful description and a question about what the next meeting might show.

An added a final example to the margin. A reader who paused because the drill was loud might need a quieter room; a reader who paused because the sentence was complex might need a different explanation. The same visible pause could therefore invite different follow-up questions.

### Questions

1. How did the group replace a fixed label with a more evidence-based question about Ben’s character?
2. Why are the commas correct in “The character, who cancelled the first meeting, sent a new message”?
3. In “the message that Linh received”, what is the role of **that**, and how would omission change the sentence?
4. Distinguish the opening clause in “Reviewing the difficult paragraph, An asked…” from the reduced clause in “The examples selected by the group…”.

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) list the two appointment changes and their conditions; (2) ask whether the clause identifies which character; (3) identify who received the message; (4) ask what the opening clause describes and which noun receives the selecting.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Write two sentences about a fictional meeting: one with a non-defining relative and one with a reduced passive clause. Add an adverbial participle opening whose subject is clear. State one interpretation that remains uncertain. Self-score evidence (0–1) and attachment accuracy (0–1).

<details><summary>Hidden key and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. The group listed the cancelled appointment and later message, then added the family duty and request for a public place. It changed the question from a type label to the conditions shaping the choices. A response may say the label was replaced with an evidence-and-context question.
2. **The character** is already identified, so the clause adds extra information and is non-defining; commas are required. Removing it leaves “The character sent a new message.”
3. **That** refers to **message** and is the object of **received**; Linh received the message. It may be omitted: “The message Linh received explained the new time.” The answer must not treat **that** as the subject.
4. “Reviewing the difficult paragraph” is an adverbial participle clause describing the circumstance of An’s asking; it does not modify An as a reduced relative. “Selected by the group” is a reduced passive relative clause modifying **examples**, which receive the selecting. The forms alone do not prove a past tense; context supplies timing.
</details>

## D23 — The description that stayed open

### Passage (fictional, 510 words)

The final review asked the writers to produce a short profile of a fictional mentor who worked with a mixed-age study group. The profile had to connect actions with conditions and end with a limitation. Nhi chose Dara, whose notebook contained questions from six meetings. She began with a sentence that read, “Dara is patient, organised, and good with people.” The sentence sounded positive, but it did not show what Dara had done.

Nhi returned to the notes that Dara had made after a meeting held in a crowded café. Several learners had spoken at once, so Dara asked each person to write one point before the group discussed it. The next meeting took place in a quiet room where the group could hear a recording. Dara allowed spoken discussion to begin immediately and asked learners to compare the recording with their notes. The two meetings showed adjustments that were connected to conditions.

Nhi drafted a fuller description: “Dara, who changes the order of discussion when the room changes, asks for evidence before accepting a broad claim.” The non-defining clause added information about Dara, while the main clause gave the profile’s central practice. Nhi then added, “The questions written after the café meeting helped the group separate observation from opinion.” The reduced passive clause identified questions that someone had written; it did not say when the writing happened by grammar alone.

The draft included a possessive detail: “Dara kept a folder whose first page listed the points that the group still needed to check.” Nhi checked the antecedent of **whose**. It was **folder**, and the noun after **whose** was **first page**. The phrase did not mean that Dara owned every point or that the page had a personality. It linked a folder with its first page. The defining clause **that the group still needed to check** identified which points were unfinished.

During peer review, Quang suggested replacing “Dara understands every learner” with “Dara asks what support would make the next step clearer.” Nhi accepted the revision because the notes showed questions and adjustments, not complete knowledge of other people. A second reviewer suggested that the profile should call Dara “introverted” because she often wrote before speaking. Nhi kept the observation but declined the label. Writing first could be a useful preparation choice in those meetings; it did not explain every future setting.

The final paragraph said that Dara’s method helped the group notice evidence, choose a next step, and revisit an earlier interpretation. It also said that one fictional profile could illustrate a practice without proving a permanent trait. Nhi read the paragraph aloud and checked that each relative clause had an antecedent, each reduced clause had a logical attachment, and each comma matched the intended meaning. The description stayed open because good evidence can guide a question without closing it too soon.

They kept the first drafts in a folder marked “revisions”, because the route from observation to description was part of the lesson. A changed sentence showed which evidence had become clearer and which conclusion still required patience.

### Questions

1. What evidence replaced Nhi’s first list of positive adjectives?
2. What does the non-defining clause in “Dara, who changes the order…” add to the profile?
3. What is the antecedent of **whose** in the folder sentence, and what does the following defining clause identify?
4. Why did Nhi keep the observation about writing first but decline the label “introverted”?

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) compare the café and quiet-room observations; (2) remove the comma clause and identify the main claim that remains; (3) find the noun before **whose**, then ask which points the **that** clause identifies; (4) distinguish a preparation choice observed in these meetings from a prediction about every setting.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Write a fictional profile that combines one non-defining **who** clause, one **whose + noun** clause, one active reduced clause, and one passive reduced clause. Include two observations under different conditions and a final limitation. Self-score evidence (0–1) and attachment accuracy (0–1).

**Additional D23 review:** Revisit the café and quiet-room notes, test the main sentence after removing its comma clause, identify the folder/first-page relationship and the unfinished points, and explain why an observed preparation choice does not establish behaviour in every setting.

<details><summary>Hidden key and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. She used the café procedure of writing one point before discussion, the quiet-room procedure of comparing a recording with notes, and the related change in discussion order. These actions and conditions replaced unsupported adjectives.
2. It adds the observed practice that Dara changes discussion order when the room changes. Because Dara is already named, the clause is non-defining and the commas signal additional information; the main clause still says she asks for evidence.
3. **Whose** refers to **folder**, and **first page** is the noun associated with it. “That the group still needed to check” is a defining clause identifying which points were unfinished. It does not modify Dara or imply that the page has a personality.
4. Writing first was observed in the meetings and could be useful preparation, but the evidence did not explain Dara’s behaviour in every future setting. Nhi kept the condition-linked observation and avoided turning it into a permanent type claim.
</details>

## Cycle-wide self-check

For each day, mark one full relative clause, one omitted-object opportunity, one non-defining clause, one **whose** clause, and one reduced clause. For every clause, name its antecedent or subject and explain the evidence it adds. Remember that a comma plus **and** can coordinate complete ideas when the conjunction is present; an opening participle can be adverbial rather than a reduced relative; **-ing** does not by itself guarantee an ongoing event; and a past participle does not by itself encode completion. Score 0–2 for each day’s transfer and record one sentence you revised.
