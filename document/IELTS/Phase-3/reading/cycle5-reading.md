# Reading — Cycle 5: an integrated description workshop

This cycle covers D17–D19 through three original fictional passages about teams that describe behaviour carefully. Each passage combines defining and non-defining relatives, **whose**, **where**, **when**, and reduced clauses. The descriptions are evidence-led but never diagnostic or predictive. Spend about 50 minutes per day: preview and glossary (5), first reading (12), four questions (15), evidence check and hints (10), and transfer writing (8). Try the questions before opening each hidden key. Grammar reference: [Grammar 5 — Personality description workshop](../grammar/05-personality-description-workshop.md).

## D17 — The profile assembled from small decisions

### Passage (fictional, 517 words)

The archive team needed a short profile of its new volunteer, Hana, who had joined during a crowded month. The coordinator, Pavel, wanted a paragraph that would help colleagues work with her. His first draft called Hana “independent, organised, and introverted”. Hana read the paragraph beside the filing cabinet where the team stored unfinished descriptions. She agreed that the words were familiar, but asked Pavel to show the observations that supported them.

Pavel opened the notes that he had collected during three shifts. On Monday, Hana had arrived early and labelled boxes that lacked dates. On Wednesday, she had asked another volunteer to check a list that she had sorted. On Friday, she had moved to the quieter table when the public tour began, then returned to the shared table when the team needed to compare names. The notes showed choices that changed with the task. They did not show a single quality that explained every choice.

Hana suggested a different profile. “Hana checks the condition of each box before entering a date. She asks for a second check when a list will affect another person. During a public tour, she works at the courtyard table, which has fewer interruptions. When the tour ends, she joins the group that compares uncertain names.” Pavel noticed that the new version was longer, but each sentence answered a practical question. The relative clause beginning **which** described the courtyard table, not Hana, and the clause beginning **that** identified the group she joined.

The team then discussed a note that Hana had written about a missing photograph. The photograph, whose envelope had no date, was placed in a temporary folder. Hana did not claim that the image belonged to a particular year. She wrote that its location was known, its date was uncertain, and the next action was to compare the handwriting with two dated envelopes. Pavel liked the possessive clause because it connected the envelope with its missing date without pretending that the date had been discovered.

A colleague, Tomas, said that the profile sounded cautious because it used too many details. Hana replied that a shorter label could be useful as a search term, provided the examples remained nearby. She proposed a heading, “checks conditions before deciding”, followed by three notes. The heading was a temporary summary; the notes were the evidence that could confirm, refine, or challenge it. The team accepted the structure and added a line explaining that behaviour observed during a busy tour might differ from behaviour observed during quiet sorting.

At the end of the week, Pavel asked whether Hana now considered herself organised. Hana said that she could recognise a repeated practice in the notes, but she would not use one week to explain all future work. The team’s final profile therefore described actions, conditions, and possible support. It did not turn a person into a list of permanent types. The paragraph was useful because a colleague could act on it: provide a second checker for uncertain records, offer a quiet table for focused sorting, and ask before assuming why a choice had been made.

### Questions

1. Why did Hana ask Pavel to show the observations behind the three labels? Give two examples of choices that changed with the task.
2. In “the notes that he had collected” and “the courtyard table, which has fewer interruptions”, identify the antecedent and grammatical role of each relative word. Which clause is non-defining?
3. What does “whose envelope had no date” express, and why did Hana avoid claiming that the photograph’s year was known?
4. How did the final profile turn description into practical support without claiming to explain Hana’s future behaviour?

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) find the three dated shifts and compare the setting; (2) identify the noun before each relative clause and ask who performed the verb; (3) look at the noun immediately after **whose** and the evidence Hana says is still uncertain; (4) list the support actions in the final paragraph and the sentence that limits prediction.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Write a fictional profile with one defining object relative, one non-defining relative with commas, and one **whose** clause. Add two observable actions under different conditions and one sentence stating what the profile cannot prove. Check that every relative clause attaches to the intended noun.

<details><summary>Hidden key, evidence, and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. Hana wanted the labels to remain accountable to observable evidence. She moved to a quieter table during the tour but returned to the shared table for name comparison; she asked for a second check on a consequential list while sorting boxes independently. Any two task-linked examples are acceptable.
2. In “the notes that he had collected”, **that** refers to **notes** and is the object of **collected**, so it can be omitted. In “the courtyard table, which has fewer interruptions”, **which** refers to **table** and is the subject of **has**; the commas mark a non-defining clause, which adds information and cannot be reduced to an omitted subject pronoun here.
3. **Whose envelope had no date** links the photograph to an envelope and identifies the envelope’s missing date. Hana avoided a year claim because the note established location and uncertainty only; she planned to compare handwriting rather than infer a date from an undated envelope.
4. It gave colleagues concrete actions—use a second checker, offer a quiet table, and ask before interpreting a choice—while describing the conditions under which the observations occurred. The temporary heading could be refined, and the profile explicitly refused to explain all future work.

</details>

## D18 — The workshop map with a missing arrow

### Passage (fictional, 505 words)

The language club designed a workshop for volunteers who often gave feedback on short stories. Its first map showed a straight route: read the story, mark the strongest paragraph, and suggest a revision. The route looked efficient, but it ignored the writers who needed to understand a question before choosing a revision. Mira, whose job was to coordinate the workshop, asked the volunteers to test the map with a fictional draft about a bus station.

The draft followed a character who waited beside a timetable that displayed two different departure times. One volunteer, Ellis, marked the opening as confusing. Another volunteer, Noor, wrote that the confusion was deliberate because the character had received two messages. Mira asked the group to identify the evidence before choosing a label. The paragraph that Ellis had marked contained the timetable; the message that Noor had noticed appeared three lines later. Neither observation alone explained the whole scene.

Mira changed the map. The first step became “name the sentence that created your question”. The second asked readers to find the detail that answered or complicated it. The third invited the writer to choose between two revisions, one that clarified the timetable and another that made the conflicting messages more visible. The volunteer who preferred a quick judgement had to record a reason. The volunteer who needed time could write before the discussion began.

During the trial, a writer called Samira chose the revision that made the messages more visible. She explained that the character’s uncertainty was part of the story, but the reader needed to know why the timetable mattered. Mira noted that the choice was made after the evidence had been compared, not because Samira had been assigned a personality type. The revision suggested a next action: add one phrase before the second message. It did not prove that Samira always preferred ambiguity.

The volunteers then examined the language on their own feedback cards. One card said, “The sentence receiving the most questions needs work.” Mira changed it to “The sentence that received the most questions needs a closer look.” The first version could suggest an action in progress, although the questions had already been counted. The second version made the completed event clearer. Another card read, “The reader waiting at the station notices the sign.” Mira kept the **-ing** clause because the reader was shown during an ongoing wait, but she checked that the reader, not the sign, was doing the waiting.

At the end of the session, the club placed the old map beside the revised map. The old route was not useless: it reminded readers to notice structure. The revised route added a question, an evidence check, and a choice that the writer could explain. Mira wrote a note whose purpose was to protect this sequence: “A useful description says what happened, where the evidence appears, and when an interpretation remains uncertain.” The note did not make every reader work in the same way. It made the reasoning visible enough for different readers to join it.

### Questions

1. What was missing from the workshop’s original straight route, and how did the revised map support both quick and careful readers?
2. Compare “the paragraph that Ellis had marked” with “the sentence receiving the most questions”. Which is a full relative clause, and what is the voice/time meaning of the reduced clause?
3. Why did Mira change the feedback card from the reduced “receiving” phrase to the full clause “that received”? What did she check in the other **-ing** example?
4. What was the purpose of the note “whose purpose was to protect this sequence”, and how did the two maps work together?

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) locate the three new map steps and the two kinds of reader; (2) ask whether the relative word is followed by a full subject-and-verb structure; (3) compare the time of counting with the time of writing the card; (4) match the note to “what, where, when” and compare the purposes of both maps.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Design a three-step feedback route. Include one full relative clause, one passive reduced clause, and one active **-ing** clause with a clear subject. Add a **whose** clause explaining the purpose of one step and state one uncertainty that the route cannot resolve.

<details><summary>Hidden key, evidence, and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. The straight route had no step for naming a question, finding the evidence that answered or complicated it, or explaining a revision choice. The revised map required a reason for quick judgements and allowed a writer who needed time to write before discussion. “It supported different pacing while keeping the evidence check” is acceptable.
2. “The paragraph that Ellis had marked” is a full relative clause; **that** is the object of **had marked**. “The sentence receiving the most questions” is a reduced active clause, with **receiving** used here for a relation during the question period. An **-ing** clause can also have a habitual meaning in another context. Mira replaced it with the full clause **that received the most questions** to state the counted past event explicitly; **received** is a finite simple-past verb in this full active clause, not a passive reduced participle.
3. The full clause **that received** uses a finite past-tense verb to match the counted event. It makes the timing explicit rather than relying on the reduced phrase. In “the reader waiting at the station”, Mira checked that **reader** was the logical subject of **waiting**, not the sign, so the reduced clause did not dangle.
4. The note preserved the sequence of event, evidence location, and uncertainty in an interpretation. The old map highlighted structure, while the revised map made reasoning and choice visible; together they retained a useful starting point and added an accountable process.

</details>

## D19 — The final description beside the open door

### Passage (fictional, 574 words)

For the final workshop task, the group had to write a 200-word description of a fictional discussion leader. The brief asked for evidence, connections between ideas, and a careful limitation. It did not ask writers to choose a personality type. The model paragraph, which sat beside the open door, described a leader who changed her questions when the group’s needs changed. The facilitator, Jo, asked everyone to inspect the grammar before copying the structure.

Leila chose to describe Noor, who led a neighbourhood reading circle. Noor prepared a list of questions that invited examples, but she removed two questions when the meeting moved from a large room to a narrow café. The café, whose tables were close together, made it difficult for several people to spread out their notes. Noor therefore asked readers to compare one passage at a time. She did not conclude that the group had become less interested; she recorded that the setting had changed the available space and time.

Leila’s first draft included this sentence: “Noor, who the members trusted, asked for examples, and the notes written during the meeting were kept.” Jo asked Leila to check the attachment. The non-defining clause **who the members trusted** correctly added information about Noor. The reduced passive clause **written during the meeting** correctly described notes that members wrote. However, the sentence joined two ideas with a comma and **and** without showing how the notes related to the request. Leila split it into two sentences and added the reason in the next line.

The revised passage said that Noor asked for examples before accepting a general comment. A member, who had initially called the discussion “unproductive”, pointed to a page whose margin contained three concrete questions. The member changed the comment after noticing that the group had produced useful questions even though it had not reached a final decision. Leila wrote that the example supported a claim about that meeting. It did not prove that Noor’s method would work in every group.

Jo then asked Leila to include one reduced active clause. Leila wrote, “The leader reviewing the final notes invited the members to choose one question for next week.” Jo checked the attachment: the leader, Noor, was reviewing the notes, so the reduced relative phrase identified the intended person. Leila also wrote, “The examples selected by the group were placed beside the questions.” This reduced passive clause identified examples that the group had selected. Jo accepted both, but asked Leila to avoid stacking so many clauses that the main action became hard to find.

The final paragraph described a change in the circle. At the first meeting, Noor spoke for most of the opening minutes. At the next meeting, she asked members to read silently before discussion because the room was smaller. The difference showed an adjustment to conditions, not a contradiction that needed a new label. Leila concluded that Noor’s observable practice was to make the purpose of a question explicit and revise the format when evidence about the setting changed. She added that one fictional account could illustrate a pattern of action without explaining a whole person.

The writers read their descriptions aloud beside the open door. They checked every antecedent, every comma, and every participle. A useful profile, Jo reminded them, should give a reader enough evidence to question an interpretation. It should also leave enough room for the person in the story to act differently in a new situation.

### Questions

1. What evidence showed that Noor adapted to the café, and what did Leila avoid concluding from that change?
2. Explain the roles of **who** in “Noor, who the members trusted” and **whose** in “a page whose margin contained three concrete questions”.
3. Why did Jo ask Leila to split her first long sentence, and how do the two reduced clauses later in the passage attach to their subjects?
4. What was the final workshop’s limitation about describing a person, and how did the writers check that their grammar supported the meaning?

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) list the café changes and the exact conclusion Leila rejected; (2) identify the noun after **whose** and the verb after **who**; (3) compare the two ideas in the first draft, then ask who is reviewing and who is selected; (4) find the final limitation and the four grammar checks named at the end.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Write a fictional 100-word profile combining one non-defining **who** clause, one **whose + noun** clause, one active reduced clause, and one passive reduced clause. Include two condition-linked observations and a final limitation about what the account cannot prove. Revise so each clause attaches to the intended noun.

<details><summary>Hidden key, evidence, and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. Noor removed two questions when the meeting moved to the narrow café, asked readers to compare one passage at a time, and recorded the reduced space and time. Leila avoided concluding that the group was less interested or that Noor had a fixed new type; she kept the explanation tied to the changed setting.
2. **Who** refers to Noor and is the object of **trusted**; the non-defining commas add information about her. **Whose** refers to **page** and comes before **margin**, expressing the association between the page and its margin. The page’s margin contains the questions; it does not mean that the page owns a person.
3. The first sentence joined Noor’s trusted status and the notes without making their relationship clear, and the two coordinated ideas obscured the main action. The comma plus **and** correctly joins two main clauses; the problem is the unclear connection, not a comma splice. Splitting it clarified the evidence and reason. In “the leader reviewing the final notes”, **reviewing** modifies **leader** (Noor), corresponding to “the leader who was reviewing the final notes”; it is an active reduced relative phrase. A fronted phrase such as “Reviewing the notes, Noor …” would instead be an adverbial participle clause. In “the examples selected by the group”, the examples receive the selecting, so the reduced clause is passive and attaches to examples.
4. One fictional account can illustrate a pattern of action without explaining a whole person or predicting future behaviour. The writers checked antecedents, commas, subject/object roles, voice, and participle attachment so that the grammar did not overstate the evidence or hide the main action.

</details>

## Cycle-wide language check

For each day, underline one defining clause, one non-defining clause, one **whose** clause, and one reduced clause. Identify the noun each clause modifies, then label active or passive voice and completed or ongoing meaning for reduced clauses. Check that a full relative clause has the intended subject/object relationship and that commas mark non-defining information. A correct answer may use a semantically equivalent sentence when the attachment and evidence remain accurate.
