# Reading — Cycle 3: traits, states, and careful interpretation

This cycle covers D9–D11 in three separate original English passages. Each passage is fictional and uses personality language as a prompt for evidence-based reading; no character is diagnosed and no label is treated as a measurement. Spend 50 minutes per day: preview and glossary (5), first reading (12), four questions (15), evidence check and hints (10), transfer writing (8). The optional glossary supports access and is not a new core vocabulary list. Aim for 3/4 independently, then use the progressive hints and explain the evidence again.

Grammar link: [whose, where, when](../grammar/03-relative-adverbs-whose.md) (when, where, whose, and preposition meaning). The cycle uses **whose** before a noun, **where** for a place relationship, and **when** for a time relationship. The mapping of Cycle 3 to the wider 24-day guide is owned by the separate guide; this file does not assign test day 12.

## D9 — The rehearsal room that changed its question

**Optional glossary:** rehearsal = buổi tập; cue = tín hiệu; reserved = dè dặt; revise = sửa lại; evidence = bằng chứng.

### Passage (fictional, 582 words)

The old town theatre was preparing a short performance about a lost suitcase. Mira joined the group two weeks before opening night. The director described her as reserved because she usually sat at the edge of the room and wrote notes while others improvised. At the first rehearsal, Mira declined the invitation to invent a character on the spot. She said she needed to understand the scene before choosing a voice. The director wrote “quiet” beside her name on the cast list, although he did not ask what kind of work helped her think.

The next afternoon, the cast rehearsed a scene in which three travellers argued beside a railway noticeboard. Mira was responsible for the noticeboard, whose handwritten departures had to make sense whenever the actors pointed at it. She noticed that the times in the draft contradicted one another. One train was said to leave at six, while a later line implied that it had already gone at five. Mira did not interrupt the actors. She marked the conflict and waited until the break, when the director asked for practical suggestions. She then proposed a sequence of times and explained why each clue needed to appear.

The proposal solved one problem but created another. The actors had been using the inconsistent times to make the argument funny, and they worried that a tidy board would remove the joke. Mira listened, then suggested that the contradiction could remain if a character deliberately read the wrong line. The director asked her to demonstrate the idea. She stepped into the scene, read the noticeboard in a dramatic voice, and paused at the exact moment the audience would notice the mistake. The cast laughed, not because Mira had suddenly become a different person, but because the structure gave her a clear place to act.

During the following rehearsal, a visiting student asked Mira to lead a warm-up game. The game required instant instructions and fast changes. Mira hesitated, lost the first round, and handed the activity back to the student. A cast member later said this proved that she lacked confidence. The director disagreed with the certainty of that conclusion. He had seen her speak clearly about a prepared problem, perform a planned cue, and struggle with a sudden game. Those observations could describe different states under different demands; they could not by themselves settle a permanent trait.

On opening night, a prop suitcase broke when its handle caught on a chair. Mira noticed the problem before the audience did. She moved the suitcase behind a bench, whispered a new cue to the actor, and pointed to the revised route. The adjustment was small, yet it prevented a pause from becoming a visible mistake. Afterwards, the director thanked her. Mira replied that the written notes had helped, but the cast had also made room for questions. She did not claim that preparation solved every difficulty. She said that knowing when she could observe, when she could test an idea, and when she needed another person’s cue made participation easier.

The director replaced “quiet” on the cast list with a fuller note: “watch what happens when the task, time, and invitation change.” The replacement was not a new label. It was a reminder to collect more than one kind of evidence before describing a person. Mira’s work had included private checking, public explanation, collaborative revision, and a quick practical response. The theatre still needed a performance, not a personality verdict.

### Questions

1. Why did Mira wait until the break before raising the timetable problem? Give two details.
2. What changed when Mira demonstrated the revised joke, and what does the passage say this does **not** prove about her?
3. Which two observations led the director to reject the claim that the warm-up proved a permanent lack of confidence?
4. In “the noticeboard, whose handwritten departures had to make sense”, what does **whose** refer to, and why would **where** be wrong there?

<details><summary>Hidden key and evidence</summary>

1. She did not want to interrupt the actors, and the director invited practical suggestions at the break; she had marked the contradiction first so she could explain it precisely.
2. A clear structure let her demonstrate the timing and the cast could see the joke; it does not prove she became a different person or that preparation explains every situation.
3. She spoke clearly about a prepared problem and performed a planned cue, while the warm-up demanded instant changes. The contrast shows task-dependent states, not a permanent verdict.
4. **Whose** refers to the noticeboard and introduces the noun “handwritten departures,” showing possession/association. **Where** needs a place relationship and cannot replace a possessive determiner before a noun.

</details>

**Scaffold and scoring:** First underline two observable details for each answer (0–2 evidence points), then explain the contrast between a trait label and a temporary state (0–2), and finally check the relative word and commas (0–2). Use the progressive hints only after a first attempt: (1) underline “did not interrupt” and “during the break”; (2) compare the two rehearsal conditions; (3) list evidence before and during the game; (4) look at the noun immediately after the blank.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Describe a fictional learner whose behaviour changes across two study tasks. Include one **whose**, one **when**, and two concrete observations; end by stating what the observations cannot prove. Revise once for comma and relative-clause accuracy.

## D10 — The survey with three unfinished columns

**Optional glossary:** survey = bảng hỏi; hesitant = do dự; anonymous = ẩn danh; pattern = khuôn mẫu; interpret = diễn giải.

### Passage (fictional, 570 words)

At Northbridge College, a student committee wanted to improve the quiet study room. Its first plan was based on a quick survey containing three adjectives: focused, sociable, and independent. Students could circle one word and add a comment if they wished. The committee expected the results to reveal which type of learner needed which facility. When the first responses arrived, the secretary, Jo, noticed that many students had circled more than one word in their comments. One person chose “independent” but asked for a weekly planning partner. Another chose “sociable” but said that noise made concentration difficult.

Jo asked the committee to read the comments before drawing a conclusion. The chair argued that the labels made the results easy to display. A bar chart could show how many students selected each word, whereas a page of comments looked untidy. Jo agreed that the chart was convenient, yet she pointed out that it hid the questions the students were answering. Someone might call herself independent because she liked choosing her own method, not because she wanted to work alone. Someone else might call himself sociable because he enjoyed exchanging explanations, not because he could study beside constant conversation.

The committee tested this possibility in a small fictional follow-up meeting. Hana, whose survey answer said “independent,” described a project in which she had designed the first draft alone. She had then asked two classmates to challenge her assumptions before submitting it. Daniel, who had selected both “sociable” and “focused,” explained that he preferred a short discussion followed by forty minutes of silence. Neither student rejected the adjective, but each attached a different meaning to it. Their examples also changed with the task: planning, checking, and memorising did not demand the same arrangement.

The chair suggested adding a question about frequency: “How often do you work alone?” Jo said this was useful but incomplete. A person might work alone because the room was crowded, because a deadline was near, or because no partner was available. Frequency described an event; it did not automatically explain a preference. The group therefore added questions about the condition, the choice, and the result. Students could report when they concentrated best, where interruptions occurred, and whose feedback they trusted for a particular assignment.

The revised form took longer to complete. Some students complained that the extra questions were less attractive than a three-word quiz. Jo replied that a quick answer was not always a simple answer. If the college bought partitions based only on the first chart, it might solve noise for one activity and make collaborative planning harder. The committee created two trial zones instead: a silent area with movable screens and a discussion table that could be reserved for a limited period. It also invited students to record what they tried and what changed.

After a month, the committee found no single “learner type.” Several students moved between zones during the week. One student who had described herself as quiet became the most active questioner during peer review. Another who had chosen sociable preferred a silent corner while revising numbers. The committee did not treat these changes as contradictions. It treated them as information about demands, choices, and available support. The final report kept the bar chart, but placed it beside selected comments and a warning: a label can organise a conversation, yet it cannot replace the evidence that gives the label meaning.

### Questions

1. Why did Jo think the bar chart hid an important distinction? Give two different meanings of “independent” or “sociable” from the passage.
2. What did the revised form ask students to record, and how did those questions improve the committee’s decision?
3. Why did the later examples not count as contradictions of the original labels?
4. In “Students could report when they concentrated best, where interruptions occurred, and whose feedback they trusted,” distinguish the three embedded interrogative clauses from relative clauses. What question does each clause answer?

<details><summary>Hidden key and evidence</summary>

1. The chart counted words but not their meanings. “Independent” could mean choosing one’s method while still seeking challenge; “sociable” could mean exchanging explanations while needing quiet for concentration.
2. They recorded conditions, choices, results, times, places, and trusted feedback. This linked a report to a situation and helped the committee trial both silent and discussion spaces.
3. The students behaved differently under different tasks or conditions; the passage presents labels as context-sensitive descriptions, not fixed predictions.
4. These are embedded interrogatives, not relative clauses: **when they concentrated best** answers “at what time?”, **where interruptions occurred** answers “in what place?”, and **whose feedback they trusted** answers “the feedback of which person?”. They do not modify an antecedent noun in this sentence. A relative example from the passage is “Hana, **whose survey answer** said ‘independent,’ …”, where **whose + noun** modifies Hana.
</details>

**Scaffold and scoring:** Mark the antecedent (if there is one) before labelling a clause (0–2), give one condition-and-result pair from the passage (0–2), and check the distinction between an embedded question and a relative clause (0–2). Progressive hints: (1) find the two comments that qualify a label; (2) locate “condition, choice, and result”; (3) compare planning, checking, and memorising; (4) ask “what time, what place, whose person?” before calling a clause relative.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Write a short recommendation for a study space using two observations, one limitation of a label, and one sentence with **where** or **when**. Add a sentence with **whose + noun** and revise for clear reference.

## D11 — The interview transcript with a missing margin

**Optional glossary:** transcript = bản chép lời; margin = lề; tentative = tạm thời; inference = suy luận; revise = chỉnh sửa.

### Passage (fictional, 561 words)

The editorial club was producing a guide for new volunteers. Priya interviewed members about how they handled difficult feedback. She planned to summarise each conversation in the margin of her transcript, but the first interview made her question that method. Leo arrived late, glanced at the recorder, and answered in short phrases. Priya wrote “defensive” beside his name. When she asked whether he preferred praise or criticism, he said, “Neither, if the comment is vague.” He then asked to see the sentence she wanted him to discuss.

The sentence came from a draft introduction. It claimed that every reliable volunteer responded calmly to correction. Leo had crossed out “every” and written “a volunteer may need time.” Priya thought his change sounded argumentative. Leo explained that the club’s previous editor had once corrected three paragraphs in a single message. He had read the message just before a deadline and answered too quickly. The editor later clarified which changes were essential and which were optional. Leo accepted the essential changes after that clarification and kept the optional ones for a later revision.

Priya asked whether this meant he disliked teamwork. Leo laughed. He said that he liked a shared task when each person knew whose decision was final at each stage. During a poster project, the designer, whose visual plan was strongest, decided the layout, while the writer decided the wording. They consulted one another when a change affected both. Leo said he became impatient only when several people edited the same sentence without saying who would choose the final version. The description sounded more precise than “defensive,” so Priya crossed out her first margin note.

The second part of the interview concerned a missed deadline. Leo had promised to check references by Friday, but he sent his comments on Monday. Priya asked what had happened. He showed her a calendar in which a family appointment and an unexpected shift were marked. He had also underestimated the time needed to verify a quotation. He admitted that the delay affected the team and suggested a remedy: in future, he would report a risk on Wednesday rather than wait until Friday. Priya recorded the delay, the causes he gave, and the proposed change separately. She did not turn the episode into proof that he was either unreliable or perfectly organised.

When the transcript was reviewed, another editor questioned Priya’s revisions. “You removed a useful personality description,” the editor said. Priya answered that a short label might be useful as a search term, but only if the notes beside it preserved the evidence. She kept a temporary tag, “asks for specific feedback,” and linked it to the sentence about “every,” the example of divided editing responsibility, and the late reference check. The tag remained open to revision because one interview could not show how Leo would respond in every setting.

The guide eventually used a dialogue box rather than a list of types. It showed an interviewer asking, “What would make this feedback actionable?” and a volunteer answering with a request for examples, priority, and timing. Priya added a note when the question was asked and where the answer changed the draft. The box did not promise that all volunteers needed the same support. It showed how a careful question could turn an impression into information that a team could use.

### Questions

1. Which three items does Priya explicitly link to the temporary tag “asks for specific feedback”? What limitation does she state about that tag?
2. Why did Leo’s late reference check not prove that he was simply unreliable? Give two contextual details and the action he proposed.
3. What did the dialogue box add that a list of personality types would not add?
4. In “the designer, whose visual plan was strongest, decided the layout,” explain the meaning of **whose**. Then express the same information in two independent sentences using “the visual plan of the designer.”

<details><summary>Hidden key and evidence</summary>

1. Priya explicitly links the tag to the sentence containing “every,” the example of divided editing responsibility, and the late reference check. She keeps it temporary because one interview cannot show how Leo would respond in every setting.
2. A family appointment and unexpected shift occupied the calendar, and he underestimated quotation-checking time; he proposed reporting a risk by Wednesday. These explain the episode without excusing its effect or proving a fixed trait.
3. It preserves the question, context, answer, and practical implication, showing how an impression can become usable information rather than predicting behaviour from a type.
4. **Whose** links the designer to the noun phrase “visual plan” and expresses association/possession. Two clear independent sentences are: “The visual plan of the designer was strongest. The designer decided the layout.” The version with a non-defining relative clause is more concise; **where** would be wrong because the relationship is not a place.
</details>

**Scaffold and scoring:** Circle the three facts explicitly linked to the tag (0–2), separate cause from effect and proposed remedy (0–2), and check the antecedent and commas in the **whose** clause (0–2). Progressive hints: (1) find the three linked items named in the review paragraph; (2) separate cause, effect, and remedy; (3) compare a label with the dialogue’s question-and-answer structure; (4) identify the noun after whose and preserve the non-defining commas.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Write a fictional interview exchange about feedback. Include one inference supported by two observable details, one **whose** clause, and one sentence with **when** or **where**. State one limitation of your interpretation and revise the punctuation.

## Cycle-wide language check

Underline one **whose + noun**, one **where**, and one **when** in each answer set. Explain the preposition meaning in at least two alternatives: *the room where we met / the room in which we met* and *the period when we revised / the period during which we revised*. Keep defining and non-defining commas accurate. A correct answer may use a semantically equivalent sentence when it identifies the same evidence.
