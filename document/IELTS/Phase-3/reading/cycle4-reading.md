# Reading — Cycle 4: omitted and reduced relative clauses

This cycle covers D13–D15 with three original fictional passages about teamwork, feedback, and personality descriptions. The texts use defining relatives, omitted object pronouns, and reduced clauses in context. They do not diagnose anyone or claim that a label predicts behaviour. Spend about 50 minutes per day: preview and glossary (5), first reading (12), four questions (15), evidence check and hints (10), and transfer writing (8). Try the questions before opening each hidden key. Grammar reference: [Grammar 4 — Omission and reduced relative clauses](../grammar/04-omission-reduced-clauses.md).

## D13 — The project board with two kinds of silence

### Passage (fictional, 578 words)

At the start of the community theatre project, the team described Nari as quiet. She rarely interrupted a meeting, and she preferred to mark suggestions on the shared board before speaking. Arun, the coordinator, worried that the group would overlook ideas that Nari had noticed. He asked her to lead the first planning review, but she requested a different arrangement: everyone would read the notes that she had collected, then each person would choose one question to discuss. The request changed the meeting without making Nari perform a louder personality.

The notes concerned a scene that the director wanted to shorten. Nari had listed the entrances that the audience might miss, the jokes that the actors could deliver without a pause, and the prop change that the stage crew had rehearsed twice. She had not written that the director was wrong. Instead, she separated observations from choices. The scene that the director wanted to cut contained a transition that confused new volunteers, but it also contained a gesture that explained why two characters trusted each other. Nari suggested keeping the gesture and moving the transition to a later exchange.

Arun asked whether this was the kind of solution that Nari always preferred. She said that the question assumed a stable preference where there was only a method that suited this problem. When the team had to select music the previous week, she had spoken early because she knew the available licences. When the group had to resolve a disagreement about costumes, she had listened first because two designers had information that she lacked. She did not reject the word “quiet”, but she wanted the team to ask what the word described in each situation.

During the review, Mai challenged a line that Nari had drafted. Mai thought the line made the actor sound certain about a decision that was still provisional. Nari thanked her and asked which evidence supported the concern. Mai pointed to the rehearsal log that she had updated after a late change. The log showed that the actor had learned the movement, but not that the audience understood its purpose. Nari replaced “the successful movement” with “the movement tested in rehearsal” and added a question for the next audience trial. The revision made the uncertainty visible rather than hiding it behind a positive adjective.

The team later compared the first audience notes with the plan. Visitors remembered the gesture, but several missed the reason for it. Arun proposed restoring the longer transition. Nari agreed, although she had earlier supported shortening the scene. She explained that the new evidence changed the decision, not her character. A teammate asked whether the label “careful” still fitted her. Nari answered that a label could be a useful shorthand if the examples beside it stayed visible. The board therefore kept a column for observations, a column for interpretations, and a column for decisions that the group might revise.

Before the next meeting, Arun wrote a short instruction: “Bring one point that you observed and one choice that you would change.” The instruction helped people who liked to speak immediately and people who needed time to write. Nari still marked several notes before talking, while Mai still tested ideas aloud. Their different habits did not prevent cooperation. The team used the evidence that each person contributed, checked the assumptions that shaped a decision, and treated a personality description as a prompt for a better question rather than as a final explanation.

### Questions

1. What two details led the team to describe Nari as quiet, and why did she ask the group to read her notes before speaking?
2. In “the notes that she had collected” and “the transition that confused new volunteers”, which relative pronoun can be omitted? Explain the grammatical difference.
3. Why did Nari replace “the successful movement” with “the movement tested in rehearsal”? What uncertainty did the new wording preserve?
4. What did the three board columns help the team separate, and how did the final instruction support different working habits?

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) underline the two early actions Nari takes; (2) identify the verb’s subject: is it already named inside the clause, or is the relative word itself the subject?; (3) compare what the rehearsal log proves with what it does not prove; (4) find the three column names and the two parts of Arun’s instruction.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Describe a fictional project member using two observable actions. Include one relative clause whose object pronoun can be omitted, one relative clause whose subject pronoun must remain, and one sentence explaining what your description cannot prove. Revise once for punctuation and clear reference.

<details><summary>Hidden key, evidence, and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. She rarely interrupted and marked suggestions before speaking. She wanted people to read first so that the discussion would begin with the same evidence and each person could choose a focused question. “She preferred written preparation” is acceptable if it is tied to the board notes and not treated as a fixed trait.
2. In “the notes that she had collected”, **that** is the object of **collected**, so it may be omitted: “the notes she had collected”. In “the transition that confused new volunteers”, **that** is the subject of **confused**, so it cannot be omitted; “the transition confused new volunteers” would change the structure. The same subject/object test applies to “the gesture that explained” and “the prop change that the crew had rehearsed”.
3. The movement had been tested by actors, but the log did not prove that the audience understood its purpose. The replacement avoids claiming success and keeps the difference between rehearsal evidence and audience evidence visible.
4. The columns separated observations, interpretations, and decisions open to revision. The instruction asked for one observation and one possible change, allowing immediate speakers and writers who need preparation to contribute. “It gave both groups a concrete entry point” is an acceptable summary.

</details>

## D14 — The rehearsal schedule that moved twice

### Passage (fictional, 592 words)

The student design team had six days to prepare an exhibition about repaired furniture. Its first schedule placed the interview team beside the workshop, where the carpenter demonstrated how damaged chairs were assessed. The group expected a calm week, but the building manager closed the workshop after a water leak. The students moved the interviews to a corridor and began recording the stories of residents who had repaired tables, lamps, and cupboards at home.

Milo, who was coordinating the display, proposed a shorter introduction. He wanted visitors to see objects being repaired rather than read a long explanation first. The introduction written by Sera, however, contained a distinction that the team needed: a chair repaired for safety was not the same as a chair altered for appearance. Milo asked Sera to keep the distinction but move the examples beside the photographs. Sera accepted the change after comparing the two versions. She said that the first draft was not sacred and that a useful edit should leave the reasoning easier to follow.

On the third day, two interviews arrived late. The residents invited to the corridor had misunderstood the revised appointment time, while the resident carrying a box of photographs had been delayed by a bus diversion. Milo did not describe either person as careless. He added a reminder to the message sent the night before and asked the team to record the time at which a change was announced. The reminder was a practical response to a communication gap; it was not evidence that every late arrival had the same cause.

The group then tested three labels for the visitors’ response cards: “patient”, “practical”, and “inventive”. The cards completed during the first hour were longer than the cards completed during the crowded afternoon. Sera pointed out that the label “patient” might describe waiting carefully for a repair demonstration, or it might describe having enough time to write. The team therefore replaced the labels with questions. Visitors could note what they waited for, which decision they made, and how the object changed. The new form produced less tidy totals but more useful descriptions.

Milo’s plan for the final wall used photographs selected by the residents. The images showing loose joints were placed beside the notes explaining how the joints had been tightened. The images showing decorative changes were placed beside comments about colour and use. In one caption, Milo wrote “the lamp restored by Mei”; in another, “the table receiving a new surface”. Sera checked the meanings. The lamp had received a completed action, so **restored** was suitable. The table was shown during an ongoing process, so **receiving** made the timing visible.

On the last morning, the manager reopened the workshop. The team could have returned to its first plan, but the corridor interviews had already changed the exhibition. Milo kept the photographs collected there and added a small map showing the temporary route. He said that the forced change had revealed how residents made decisions in different places. Sera replied that it had also revealed a weakness in their first description of the visitors: the team had tried to name a quality before asking what the visitors actually did.

The exhibition opened with two schedules displayed side by side. One showed the plan made before the leak; the other showed the decisions made after each new observation. The display did not celebrate disruption for its own sake. It showed that a team can adjust a plan, choose a reduced phrase carefully, and keep a claim proportional to the evidence that supports it.

### Questions

1. Why did the team change its visitor labels into questions, and what did it lose by doing so?
2. Explain the difference between “the introduction written by Sera” and “the table receiving a new surface”. Identify the voice and time meaning of each reduced clause.
3. What did the team infer from the late interviews, and what did Milo refuse to infer about the residents?
4. Why were the two schedules displayed together? Give one way this supported the passage’s view of teamwork.

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) compare the first labels with the three replacement questions; (2) ask who performed each participle’s action and whether the action is complete or ongoing; (3) separate what was recorded from the label the team declined to apply; (4) look at what each schedule proves when viewed beside the other.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Write a fictional exhibition caption containing one passive reduced relative clause and one active **-ing** reduced clause. Add two sentences explaining what the captions show and what they cannot prove about the people involved. Check that each participle has a logical subject.

<details><summary>Hidden key, evidence, and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. Labels such as “patient” could have different meanings depending on whether someone waited for a demonstration or had time to write. The questions asked what visitors waited for, which decision they made, and how an object changed. The team lost tidy totals or easy comparison, but gained more contextual descriptions. “It traded neat counts for usable explanations” is acceptable.
2. “The introduction written by Sera” is a reduced passive relative clause: Sera wrote it, and the completed draft is being identified. “The table receiving a new surface” is a reduced active clause: the table is shown during an ongoing process. **Written** expresses passive voice; **receiving** expresses active voice and, in this photograph context, an action in progress. The participle alone does not encode past tense or prove completion: the surrounding sentence identifies an existing draft. An **-ing** clause can also describe a repeated action in another context. Neither phrase is an embedded question.
3. They inferred that a communication gap affected the appointments and added a reminder plus a time record. Milo refused to infer that every late resident was careless or had one fixed cause. A response must mention both the practical inference and the rejected personality judgment.
4. The schedules made the original plan and evidence-led revisions visible together. They showed that collaboration involved changing decisions when conditions changed, while preserving the reasons for each change. “The display turned revision into shared evidence” is an acceptable alternative.

</details>

## D15 — The feedback room with a second doorway

### Passage (fictional, 549 words)

The peer-feedback group met in a room that had one large table and two doors. The first door opened onto a busy stairwell; the second opened onto a quiet courtyard. At the first meeting, members chose seats quickly. Some participants faced the stairwell because they wanted to hear people arriving. Others faced the courtyard because they needed fewer interruptions while reading. The facilitator, Linh, wrote “outgoing” beside the first group and “reserved” beside the second, then crossed out both words before the meeting ended.

Linh had asked each member to bring a paragraph that described a difficult collaboration. The paragraph that Tuan submitted concerned a poster designed by four students. Tuan wrote that he had spoken often during early planning, but he had stopped contributing when the group began changing the same sentence without recording the reason. The draft suggested that Tuan was inconsistent. Linh asked him to describe the sequence instead. He explained that he had proposed three layouts, accepted a different layout, and then asked for a single editor when the captions became confusing. His behaviour changed as the task changed.

The paragraph that Mei brought described a different problem. Mei had been the person who checked every citation, but she had overlooked a date in a source list assembled near midnight. She corrected the date the next morning and told the group how the mistake had happened. A member called her “careful”, while another member said that one missed detail proved the label was wrong. Linh asked both members to revise their statements. A useful description had to include the checking habit, the missed date, the correction, and the conditions surrounding the mistake. It did not need to choose between praise and blame.

During the second round, the members used cards that separated observation, interpretation, and next action. The card selected by Tuan asked, “Which change made your contribution less clear?” He answered that unclear ownership had made him repeat decisions. Mei selected a card asking, “When did your checking process become rushed?” She pointed to the late-night source list and added that a shared deadline could have been negotiated earlier. Linh selected a card asking, “Where would a quieter review help?” The group suggested the courtyard for reading and the large table for resolving wording together.

The cards changed the conversation without proving that one room suited everyone. Tuan still enjoyed lively planning when the decision owner was named. Mei still preferred a quiet review for citations but liked explaining corrections at the large table. The group’s notes therefore recorded conditions, choices, results, and proposed adjustments. They used the words “speaks early”, “checks detail”, and “asks for ownership” as temporary search terms. Each term was linked to examples and could be changed after another project.

At the final meeting, Linh placed two drafts beside the doorway. The first was a description full of labels: “outgoing members lead, reserved members check.” The second described actions and conditions: “Members contribute in different ways when ownership, noise, and time pressure change.” The group kept the second draft. It was longer, but it allowed a person to recognise a useful habit without being trapped by a category. The two doors remained open, and members chose where to read, discuss, or revise according to the work in front of them.

### Questions

1. Why did Linh cross out “outgoing” and “reserved”? Use two details from the room or the members’ later actions.
2. In “the paragraph that Tuan submitted” and “the person who checked every citation”, which relative pronoun is an object and which is a subject? State whether either can be omitted.
3. How did the three feedback cards change the discussion? Identify the information each card sought.
4. Compare the two final drafts. Why did the group prefer the longer description, and what limitation did it preserve?

**Visible hints before answering:** (1) compare each label with the concrete behaviour that follows; (2) identify who performed **submitted** and **checked**; (3) match each card to change, time, or place; (4) find the sentence saying that terms could be changed after another project.

**Transfer (80–120 words):** Write a fictional feedback note with one omitted object relative, one subject relative that must remain, and one reduced clause. Describe a behaviour under a named condition, then add a sentence limiting what the observation can prove. Revise for logical participle attachment.

<details><summary>Hidden key, evidence, and acceptable alternatives</summary>

1. The seat choice reflected interruptions and reading needs, not a permanent type. Tuan spoke often in early planning but asked for one editor when ownership became unclear; Mei checked citations carefully but made and corrected a date error under late pressure. Any two details with this context support the answer.
2. In “the paragraph that Tuan submitted”, **that** is the object of **submitted** and can be omitted: “the paragraph Tuan submitted”. In “the person who checked every citation”, **who** is the subject of **checked** and must remain. “The person checked every citation” changes the structure and does not identify the person in the intended way.
3. Tuan’s card sought the change that made contribution unclear; Mei’s asked when checking became rushed; Linh’s asked where a quieter review would help. Together they moved the group from labels to a concrete change, time condition, place, and next action.
4. The longer draft named actions and conditions instead of treating categories as predictions. It preserved the limitation that the temporary search terms could change after another project; it did not claim that one room or one description suited everyone.

</details>

## Cycle-wide language check

For each day, mark one full subject relative and one full object relative, rewrite the object relative with its pronoun omitted, and identify one reduced clause. The passages do not promise that an omitted object relative already appears in each day’s text. Explain whether the relative word is a subject or object before omitting it. For reduced clauses, name active or passive voice and whether the context presents an ongoing or completed action. Do not label the clauses beginning **when**, **where**, or **why** as relatives unless they modify an antecedent noun; in this cycle, those words may introduce time, place, or reason information. Accept a semantically equivalent answer when the grammatical relation and passage evidence remain accurate.
