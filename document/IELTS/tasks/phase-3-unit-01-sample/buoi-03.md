## 🧠 Slot 1 — Grammar (60'): Defining vs Non-Defining

### Quy tắc dấu phẩy

| Loại | Dấu phẩy | Nghĩa | Ví dụ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Defining** (xác định) | KHÔNG | Thông tin CẦN — bỏ đi thì không biết nói ai/cái gì | My friend **who lives in Hue** is a psychologist. (tôi có nhiều bạn → chỉ rõ bạn ở Huế) |
| **Non-defining** (bổ sung) | CÓ (hai bên) | Thông tin THÊM — bỏ đi câu vẫn rõ | My sister, **who lives in Hue**, is a psychologist. (chỉ có 1 chị — thêm chi tiết) |

> **Test nhanh:** bỏ mệnh đề đi — câu còn rõ nghĩa không? Còn → non-defining (có dấu phẩy). Không → defining (không dấu phẩy).

### 3 quy tắc đi kèm

1. **Sau dấu phẩy KHÔNG dùng "that"** — chỉ who/which/whose/where/when: *My boss, **who** rarely smiles, remembered my name.* (❌ *that rarely smiles*)
2. **Tên riêng + danh từ riêng thường đi với non-defining:** *Costa, **who** co-developed the Big Five, worked with McCrae.*
3. **Defining cho phép that; non-defining không.** Đây là dấu hiệu nhận biết nhanh nhất khi đọc.

### Bài tập C (làm ra giấy — đáp án cuối file)

**C1. Thêm hoặc bỏ dấu phẩy (quyết định defining vs non-defining):**
1. The test that measures neuroticism takes ten minutes.
2. Costa who developed the Big Five with McCrae published his work over decades.
3. My colleague who is easygoing always gets the difficult clients.
4. Conscientiousness which involves self-discipline predicts job success.
5. The year when I failed that exam changed how I study.

> *Gợi ý: Câu 1 — có nhiều loại test → cần xác định. Câu 2 — Costa đã xác định sẵn bằng tên → thêm chi tiết. Câu 3 — tùy số lượng đồng nghiệp của bạn.*

**C2. Chọn who/which/that đúng (nhớ: sau dấu phẩy không có that):**
1. My laptop, ______ I bought in 2021, still works perfectly.
2. People ______ oversleep every day usually underestimate travel time.
3. Dweck, ______ wrote about mindset, inspired this unit's reading.
4. The questionnaire ______ the clinic uses is anonymous.

**C3. Viết 6 câu về BẠN hoặc người thật:** 3 defining + 3 non-defining, dùng ít nhất 4 đại từ khác nhau (who/which/that/whose/where/when). Danh sách tên riêng → non-defining.

✅ **Tiêu chí đạt Slot 1:** C1-C2 ≥80% · 6 câu C3 có dấu phẩy đúng loại.

## 📝 Slot 2 — Từ vựng (60'): 8 phrasal verbs "tính cách trong hành động"

| Cụm /IPA/ | Nghĩa | Câu ví dụ (dùng relative clause) |
|-----------|-------|-----------------------------------|
| **stand out** /stænd aʊt/ | nổi bật | The trait **that** makes him stand out is honesty. |
| **fit in** /fɪt ɪn/ | hòa nhập | Introverts fit in quietly in groups **where** listening matters. |
| **come across as** /kʌm əˈkrɒs əz/ | gây ấn tượng là | People **who** talk fast often come across as aggressive. |
| **bring out (the best in)** /brɪŋ aʊt/ | khơi lên (điều tốt nhất) | A good mentor is a person **who** brings out the best in you. |
| **live up to** /lɪv ʌp tuː/ | đạt đến kỳ vọng | He is someone **whose** work lives up to his promises. |
| **put up with** /pʊt ʌp wɪð/ | chịu đựng | Patience is the ability **which** helps you put up with slow progress. |
| **open up** /ˈoʊpən ʌp/ | cởi mở, kể chuyện lòng | Friends **whom** you trust make it easier to open up. |
| **let down** /let daʊn/ | làm thất vọng | A reliable colleague is one **who** never lets the team down. |

### Cách học (60 phút)
1. Nghe + đọc to từng cụm 3 lần (chú ý trọng âm của particle: *stand **OUT***, *let **DOWN***).
2. Che cột tiếng Anh → nhìn nghĩa nói lại cụm (10 lượt).
3. Viết đoạn **180-200 từ**: "The person who brings out the best in me" — dùng ≥4 cụm mới.
4. 8 thẻ Anki (tag `P3-M1`; phrasal verb = 1 thẻ/cụm).

### Bài tập nhanh (đáp án cuối file)
1. Her calm ______ the best in her students.
2. I can't ______ his constant complaining anymore.
3. In that loud team, the quiet analyst still ______.
4. He promised the report by Friday and he never ______ us ______.

✅ **Tiêu chí đạt Slot 2:** recall ≥6/8 · đoạn 180-200 từ có ≥4 cụm.

## 📖 Slot 4 — Đọc (50') + Hoạt động: tự làm Big Five thật

### Bài 3 — The Four Letters That Took Over the Office (~510 từ)

<!-- read-start -->
Every hiring season, someone in the office discovers the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator — MBTI, the test that sorts people into sixteen types using four letters: E or I, S or N, T or F, J or P. Within a week, email signatures announce it. The introverted engineer is an INTP; the cheerful manager is an ENFJ; and someone who can never find their keys declares that this explains everything. The four letters are fun, easy to remember, and everywhere. There is only one problem: in personality research, the four letters gradually lost.

The critique is not secret. David Pittenger, whose 1993 review remains one of the most cited papers on the topic, summarized the evidence bluntly: measured again and again, a large share of MBTI takers get a different type after only a few weeks. A system whose results change when nothing about you has changed cannot measure a stable trait. Researchers also point to a deeper problem: MBTI forces each dimension into two categories, while the data keeps showing a normal distribution — most people sit near the middle of every line, not at the poles. Cutting a continuum in half creates a border that the science does not support.

Then there is the Barnum effect, the reason horoscopes feel personal. Descriptions like "you are independent yet value connection" fit almost everyone; we read them and whisper, amazed, "that is exactly me." A test whose descriptions flatter every reader will feel accurate whether or not it measures anything. Psychologists call this validation by emotion, and it is powerful — which is exactly why it is not evidence. It is the same mechanism that made astrology endure for millennia. Popularity also feeds itself: once your coworkers talk in four letters, refusing the language can feel like refusing the conversation itself — one more reason the letters survived in offices long after they lost their place in journals.

Meanwhile, the Big Five quietly won the research argument for unglamorous reasons. Its dimensions come from data — from the words people actually use to describe each other, collected across thousands of studies. Its scores sit on the continua where people actually are. Its results are reasonably stable across years, and they predict real outcomes, from job performance to health habits, better than four letters ever did. It is less romantic than MBTI — no sixteen types, no ideal match — but it measures. In a field that changes slowly, that quiet kind of winning is the only kind that lasts.

Does this mean the four letters are useless? Not exactly. The MBTI conversation gets colleagues talking about differences without blame: the planner learns that the improviser is not lazy, and the improviser learns that the planner is not controlling. As a language for discussion, it works. As a measurement, it does not — and confusing the two is how a good conversation turns into a bad decision. Use the letters to open the discussion; use the Big Five, whose evidence is real, when something important depends on the answer.

<!-- read-end -->

**Câu hỏi:**
