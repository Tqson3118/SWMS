## 🧠 Slot 1 — Grammar (60'): when / where / whose / whom

### Bảng mở rộng

| Thay cho | Dùng | Ví dụ (chủ đề tính cách) |
|---|---|---|
| "in/at/on which" (thời gian) | **when** | I still remember the moment **when** I realized I prefer quiet weekends. |
| "in/at which" (nơi chốn) | **where** | The office **where** I feel comfortable is one **where** people listen. |
| "of which / belonging to" (sở hữu) | **whose** | She is a leader **whose** calm steadies the whole team. |
| Tân ngữ chỉ người (trang trọng) | **whom** | The mentor **whom** I admire most never raises his voice. |

> **whose** = "của người/vật đó". Đứng TRƯỚC danh từ: *whose calm*, *whose patience*, *whose sense of humor*. Khác hoàn toàn **who's** = who is / who has.

### So sánh nhanh — 3 câu cùng cấu trúc

- *The year **when** we met* (= in that year)
- *The house **where** she grew up* (= in that house)
- *The woman **whose** diary he found* (= her diary)

### Lỗi kinh điển

- ❌ *The book **who** changed my mind...* → ✅ *The book **that/which** changed my mind...*
- ❌ *She's a person **who** calm I admire.* → ✅ *She's a person **whose** calm I admire.*
- ❌ *The year **where** I started therapy...* → thời gian dùng **when**, không dùng where.
- ❌ *whose* đứng một mình: *The man whose I met* → ✅ *The man **whose son** I met.*

### Bài tập B (làm ra giấy — đáp án cuối file)

**B1. Điền when / where / whose / whom:**
1. I remember the afternoon ______ I first took a personality test.
2. The therapist ______ office is near the lake specializes in anxiety.
3. He is a man ______ word is enough for the whole team.
4. The colleague ______ I interviewed yesterday turned out to be very witty.
5. That was the year ______ my patience finally grew up.

**B2. Nối 2 câu thành 1 (when/where/whose):**
1. I visited a clinic. The clinic's waiting room felt like a living room. → I visited a clinic ______.
2. There was a summer. My confidence collapsed that summer. → There was a summer ______.
3. She has a grandmother. Everyone in the village respects the grandmother's honesty. → She has a grandmother ______.

**B3. Chọn who's hoặc whose:**
1. ______ coming to the team-building day? (who's / whose)
2. The writer ______ book on habits sold millions is not a psychologist. (who's / whose)
3. ______ been introverted all his life. (who's / whose)

**B4. Viết 4 câu về BẠN:** 1 câu when (kỷ niệm), 1 câu where (nơi bạn cảm thấy là chính mình), 2 câu whose (1 người thật — ví dụ người có sự kiên nhẫn mà bạn ngưỡng mộ).

| Từ /IPA/ | Loại | Nghĩa | Câu ví dụ (dùng relative clause) |
|----------|------|-------|-----------------------------------|

| **questionnaire** /ˌkwestʃəˈner/ | noun | bảng câu hỏi khảo sát | The questionnaire **that** we filled in had 50 items. |

### Bài tập nhanh (đáp án cuối file)

2. The test placed him on the ______ between shy and sociable.

## 📖 Slot 4 — Đọc (50')

### Bài 2 — Traits Are Not Boxes (~550 từ — tiểu luận viết riêng cho lộ trình)

<!-- read-start -->
Last year, a student told me that she had finally "found herself" in a personality test: "I'm an introvert. Now everything makes sense." I understood the relief. A category can feel like a home, especially in years when you are still deciding who to be. But the more I read about personality, the more I believe that the test's most honest result is not the four-letter label at the end. It is the sentence people rarely read: your personality describes your habits, not your limits.

Psychologists separate traits from states for a good reason. Traits are the stable patterns — the long rivers that run under a life. States are the weather above them: the confidence you feel before a presentation, the courage that appears in an emergency, the patience you borrow when a child asks the same question nine times. Brian Little, the psychologist whose research on "free traits" has become famous, gives a name to what my student did not expect: introverts can act extroverted for something they love, and extroverts can sit in silence for a project they believe in. The cost is real — acting against your own character is tiring — but the ability is also real.

This is where the famous "growth mindset" research by Carol Dweck meets personality science. A fixed mindset treats a trait as a box: I am bad at public speaking, therefore I will never speak. A growth mindset treats the same trait as a starting point: I am someone whose heart races before a talk, and I can still learn to speak well with a racing heart. Notice that the second sentence does not deny the trait. It denies the verdict. Teachers see this every term: two students with the same shy disposition, one sentence apart, end up in completely different rooms a year later — the one who let the trait decide stayed home, while the one who only described it is standing at the front of a seminar, still shy, still nervous, and speaking anyway.

There is a practical test for the difference. Ask: does this sentence describe me, or does it decide for me? "I need quiet time to recharge" describes — and it is useful knowledge, because the person who knows this can plan recovery after a loud week. "I'm an introvert, so I can't lead meetings" decides — and it quietly closes a door that was never actually locked. The first sentence is self-knowledge. The second is a self-fulfilling prophecy wearing the costume of science.

None of this means traits are illusions. They are among the most stable things psychology has measured, and pretending they do not exist is another kind of self-deception. The mature position is quieter than either extreme: know your defaults, respect their cost, and remember that every trait has a range. The shy student who speaks in a seminar is still shy — she is shy and brave in the same five minutes. That is not a contradiction. That is what a person, as opposed to a category, looks like.

So keep your test results. Read them like a weather report, not like a sentence. And when a label starts making decisions for you, remember the sentence people rarely read: your personality describes your habits, not your limits.
